Gate Review 2 · Concept & Prototyping

Downselection

The team selected the refined Rolling Compression Station (A′): Concept A plus two features borrowed from the CubeClamp (E). It scored highest in the weighted matrix, 3.26 of 5, and held first place under most alternative weightings. The matrix alone could not separate it from the CubeClamp; buildability decided the choice [4].

Concepts, datum and process

The five top concepts were evaluated: A, Rolling Compression Station; B, Three-Prong Fork; C, Instrumented Grip Glove; D, Instrumented Test Gun; and E, CubeClamp. D, the test gun, is the datum for both matrices. It is the most generic objective tester, one handheld device, one controlled squeeze and one reading, so using it keeps the reference neutral rather than a team favorite.

  1. Feasibility screen against the hard constraints: no ball damage, a prototype under $400, buildable this semester, and a test of 30 s or less. No concept was eliminated, but three were flagged. B's ±1.25 mm ball-diameter tolerance causes about ±40% error in squash; C's sensor error (±10–25%) is as large as the softening signal; and E's 40 mm opening cannot admit the 74 mm ball as drawn.

Criteria and weights

The criteria are the eleven customer needs in the team QFD, used unchanged, so each score traces to a need and its specification [1]. Each need's weight comes from how the four stakeholders rank it. Each rank was converted to points (rank 1 = 11 points, rank 11 = 1), multiplied by the stakeholder weight recorded in the QFD, summed, normalized to 100% and rounded to whole percentages. The stakeholder weights are Player 55% (primary user and buyer), Producer 25% (decides whether the product can be built and sold at the target price), and Court owner and Other players 10% each (affected by test time, but neither buys nor builds it).

The performance needs (identify, no damage, repeatable, confidence) carry 51% of the weight, which matches the QFD finding that customer value comes from detection and confidence. Two pairs of criteria could double-count, so each was defined to measure a different quantity: tests quickly versus fits existing routine (seconds versus number of user actions), and identifies versus repeatable (accuracy versus spread between tests and operators).

Unweighted, the Pugh matrix ties the Glove (C) and CubeClamp (E) at +1, with A level with the datum. Because it counts every criterion equally, the Glove's convenience wins cancel its losses on detection. That is why the weighted matrix is needed: a tester that cannot tell a worn ball from a good one fails, however convenient it is. Only A and E beat the datum on all three top player needs; B and C each lose on a heavily weighted need that redesign cannot easily fix.

Combining concepts: A′

Following the Pugh combine-and-improve step, two of E's features were added to A at almost no cost, because A already measures force and plate position. Hysteresis: the force–deflection loop from each squeeze is used as a second damage signal. Steel-ball calibration: flex in the frame and load cell is measured with a steel ball and subtracted. The refined concept is called A′.

Evidence for the deciding scores

  • Identification. A worn ball's stiffness drops about 10% per hour of play, 93.5 to 75.2 lb/in over two hours [2]. A tests 4–6 positions, catching local soft spots that one squeeze misses. E reads stiffness, hysteresis and bulge, but only along one axis. A′ earns a 5 because it is the only concept with several damage signals at several positions. B's diameter error (about ±40%) and C's sensor error (±10–25%) are as large as the signal.
  • No damage. A's 5 mm hard stop is below the 0.25 in. (6.35 mm) displacement of the ASTM F1888 compression test that the USA Pickleball standard uses [3], but A squeezes several times per test, so it rates the same as the datum. E's cups spread the load; B's prong tips concentrate it at hole edges.
  • Repeatability and confidence. A and E are motorized, so the user's force does not affect the result, and both show the test happening. C depends on each user's grip.
  • Cost, size and speed. A and E each need two motors and a benchtop frame: about $130 and $200 to prototype (team estimates), and both exceed 2.5 L. A takes 20–30 s with 2 actions. E takes about 15 s but 4 actions, because the ball does not fit through its opening.

Avoiding false precision and bias

  • Whole-number ratings and whole-percent weights; every rating had to cite a mechanism or a number.
  • A neutral datum, with criteria and weights fixed from the QFD before scoring.
  • Pugh and weighted ratings were cross-checked for direction; five mismatches were corrected, mostly on B and C.
  • A′ is shown beside the original A, so the effect of the refinement is visible.

Sensitivity

The matrix reliably narrows the field to A′ and E but cannot separate them alone: their 0.07-point gap is smaller than one rating step on a top criterion.

  • Stakeholder weighting. Re-scored with each stakeholder alone, equal stakeholder weights, and equal criterion weights, A′ ranks first or tied for first in 7 of 8 cases. The exception is court owners alone, where the Glove and CubeClamp tie.
  • Single changes. In 12 of 110 one-step rating changes, E overtakes A′, all on heavily weighted criteria. Halving the identification weight ties A′ and E; doubling the damage weight puts E ahead.

When the matrix and judgment disagreed

The first matrix ranked the original A (3.11) below E (3.19). The team checked each possible cause:

  • Missing criteria: none. All eleven QFD needs were used, and producer constraints were applied in the feasibility screen.
  • Weights or criteria improperly defined: no. They come directly from the QFD and were not changed to favor A.
  • Scores lacking evidence: five mismatched ratings were fixed, mostly on B and C. One correction helped E.
  • Key risks not represented: yes. The matrix scores performance once a concept works, not the risk of making it work. E has three unresolved build problems (loading, motor synchronization, flexure); A has none of that size.
  • More research or prototyping: yes. Rather than rescore A, the team combined E's strengths into A (A′) and assigned the remaining A′-versus-E uncertainty to prototype testing.

The other three each fail a top need: B, Fork (2.66), because its ball-diameter error defeats identification; C, Glove (2.77), because its sensor error and grip variation defeat identification and repeatability; and D, Gun (3.00), because it tests only one spot.

Needs that drove the decision. Identification (15%), no damage (13%), repeatability (13%) and confidence (10%) carry half the weight. Their specifications ask for at least 90% agreement with the bounce test, 0.025 mm resolution, under 10% variation between operators, and at most 0.5 in. of rebound change after 50 tests. Only motorized compression concepts can plausibly meet these, and the same method is already used to test tennis, golf and baseballs.

Where A′ outperforms. It is the only concept that measures several damage signals at several positions. Softening shows as lower stiffness and a wider loop, and a local soft spot shows as spread between positions, which a one-axis squeeze (D, E) cannot see. Compared with E, it needs half the user actions and has no loading, motor-synchronization or flexure-machining problem. With the matrix nearly tied, the concept that is cheaper and faster to prove is the better engineering choice.

Features taken from other concepts. From E: hysteresis sensing, steel-ball calibration, and a visible test that builds trust (the windowed door of the DinkCheck housing). From B: an outlier check, in which a position reading far from the others flags a ball sitting on a hole or seam. From C: a single Playable / Non-playable result instead of raw numbers.

Weak points and risks. Size and cost: the frame exceeds the 2.5 L / 2 kg targets, and two motors make the $33 unit cost hard; detailed design should shrink the frame and test one motor for both functions. Coverage: one rotation axis tests about 30% of the surface. Calibration: per-model thresholds are not yet set, and readings are temperature-sensitive.

Why it is viable. All parts are off the shelf, at an estimated $130 against the $400 budget. The 5 mm stop stays below the USA Pickleball compression-test depth. Third-party force-gauge testing showed a linear force–displacement response, supporting stiffness as a usable signal [2]. The alpha prototype already shows the two-roller cradle at full scale.

Recommendation: proceed with A′ into detailed design

First validation steps: (1) drop-test 20 new and used balls to confirm at least 90% agreement with the bounce test; (2) run 50 tests on control balls to confirm at most 0.5 in. of rebound change; (3) measure roller slip and test time on the built station. If agreement falls short, the CubeClamp's curve-based approach is the fallback.
