Section 03b

Characteristics & Targets

Each product characteristic, the need it serves, how it is measured, its preliminary target, and the evidence behind it.

QFD Survey n=36 Interviews n=4 Benchmarking

Types. A requirement must be met; an objective is maximized or minimized; a constraint bounds the design absolutely. Direction: ▲ increase is better, ▼ decrease is better. Numeric targets come from the team QFD [3]; each characteristic's evidence names its source type (survey, interview, benchmarking, standard, or business analysis).

1. Reduction in games affected by ball failure

Customer needConsistent performance; durability
DefinitionHelps reduce the number of games where a ball problem affects play.
Metric and unitsDecrease in games with equipment malfunction (%)
Direction▲ increase
TypeObjective
Preliminary target53% decrease (QFD)
EvidenceSurvey: 52.8% rank consistency first; the QFD set a matching 53% target [1][3]
RelationshipsDepends on how accurately the product identifies balls that are becoming unreliable.
Tradeoff / riskIf too sensitive, it could tell players to replace balls that are still usable.

2. Player confidence

Customer needConfidence in ball quality
DefinitionPlayers feel more confident that a ball is ready to use after testing it.
Metric and unitsUser confidence in equipment quality (%), self-rated on the survey’s 7-point Likert question, before and after testing
Direction▲ increase
TypeObjective
Preliminary target53% (QFD), set from the 52.8% of respondents who rate their confidence 3 or lower [3]
EvidenceSurvey: 52.8% rate their confidence 3 or lower of 7 [1]
RelationshipsA clear, easy-to-read result makes the product more useful.
Tradeoff / riskToo much information could confuse users instead of increasing confidence.

3. Testing time

Customer needQuick and convenient testing
DefinitionHow long it takes to test one pickleball.
Metric and unitsSeconds per test (s)
Direction▼ decrease
TypeRequirement
Preliminary target≤ 30 s (QFD)
EvidenceBenchmarking: Go/No-Go under 60 s per paddle [5]; Paddle Press and Head 4-in-1 times not published. Interview: manual check about 3 s (P3) [2]. Stakeholders affected by delay [3]
RelationshipsMore tests or measurements add information but also add time.
Tradeoff / riskBalancing how much information the product gives against how long users wait.

4. Product cost

Customer needAffordable price
DefinitionWhat the finished product would cost the customer.
Metric and unitsDollars per unit ($)
Direction▼ decrease
TypeRequirement
Preliminary target≤ $100 (QFD)
EvidenceBenchmarking: instruments cost $2,900 (Head 4-in-1) [10] to $4,995 (Paddle Press) [9] to buy; the Go/No-Go rents for $299 a month [6]; business analysis pending
RelationshipsDriven by the number of components, sensors, materials and test capabilities.
Tradeoff / riskMore capability raises cost and makes the product less attractive.

5. Product weight

Customer needPortability
DefinitionTotal weight of the tester.
Metric and unitsKilograms (kg)
Direction▼ decrease
TypeConstraint
Preliminary target≤ 7.5 kg (QFD)
EvidenceBenchmarking: Go/No-Go 12 lb (≈5.4 kg) [5]; Head 4-in-1 ships at 22.8 kg, so the machine weighs less [11]
RelationshipsMore sensors, structure or test hardware add weight.
Tradeoff / riskA lighter product may limit what it can measure.

6. Ball damage during testing

Customer needNo damage from testing
DefinitionWhether using the tester damages the ball.
Metric and unitsTested equipment that maintains or improves time to failure (%)
Direction▲ increase
TypeRequirement
Preliminary target≥ 95% (QFD); team text states 0% damage in normal testing
EvidenceQFD: top-four requirement for every stakeholder [3]
RelationshipsDepends on force applied, how the ball is held, and how the test is performed.
Tradeoff / riskA stronger test could give more information but raise the chance of damage.

7. Test repeatability

Customer needReliable, objective results
DefinitionWhether repeated tests of the same ball give similar results.
Metric and unitsMeasurement variation from repeated tests, as % of the standard’s bounce tolerance (%GRR)
Direction▼ decrease
TypeObjective
Preliminary target< 10% of the 4 in. bounce window (30–34 in.), i.e. ≤ 0.4 in.; 10–30% marginal
EvidenceStandard: AIAG measurement-system guidance, under 10% adequate, over 30% unacceptable [7]; bounce window from the USA Pickleball standard [8]; survey: current checks are subjective [1]
RelationshipsDepends on the test method, sensors and how consistently the ball is positioned.
Tradeoff / riskA tightly controlled test may be harder or slower to use.

8. Ease of use

Customer needSimple testing; requires minimal changes to existing practices
DefinitionHow easily someone performs a test and understands the result.
Metric and unitsUsers who complete a test without assistance (%)
Direction▲ increase
TypeObjective
Preliminary targetMost first-time users complete a test without help; percentage to be set from prototype testing
EvidenceInterview: club venues often run with no staff to help (P4) [2]
RelationshipsLinked to testing time and how the result is presented.
Tradeoff / riskExtra features can make the product harder to understand.

9. Ball compatibility

Customer needCompatible with common balls
DefinitionThe range of pickleball models that can be tested.
Metric and unitsCommon ball models supported (number or %)
Direction▲ increase
TypeRequirement
Preliminary targetAll five balls listed in the survey: Franklin X-40, Onix Dura Fast 40, Life Time LT Pro 48, Vulcan VPRO Flight, Selkirk Pro S1
EvidenceSurvey: brand options offered; Franklin X-40 most used (38.9%) [1]; interviews: clubs standardize on Life Time balls (P3, P4) [2]; 393 approved balls [4]
RelationshipsSupporting more balls makes the product more useful.
Tradeoff / riskIt could also make the design more complicated.

How well supported are the targets?

Targets for confidence, malfunction reduction, time, price, weight and footprint rest on survey data or competitor benchmarking. Repeatability now follows a published measurement-system criterion. Targets for carrying capacity, tracking, ease of use and durability still rest on team judgment and are the weakest.

Primary drivers of success

Customer value comes from malfunction reduction and user confidence. Market differentiation comes from price, weight and testing time, the attributes on which every existing instrument fails as a courtside product. Technical feasibility will be decided by whether the no-damage and repeatability targets can be met within the price and weight targets.
