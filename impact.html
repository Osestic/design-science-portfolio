Gate Review 3 · Social Impact Statement

Social, ethical and environmental impact of the MVP

CuPickle's largest possible benefit is fewer good balls thrown away and fewer bad ones kept; its largest possible harm is a wrong grade presented with false authority, followed by a finger pinch at the press. Those two concerns shaped the GR3 design more than any other social or environmental factor: they led to the three-state result, the door interlock and the limits placed on product claims. Environmentally, 91.5% of CuPickle's life-cycle energy is in its materials, most of it in the circuit board and display, so a longer product life and a simpler display matter more than operating energy.

This updates the Gate Review 2 statement for the detailed MVP: its peg and single press, its materials, its manufacturing plan, its battery and its end-of-life plan.

The most important professional duty is the accuracy of the grade. Until blind testing shows ≥ 90% agreement with the bounce test, the reference-ball method is a starting point and is not presented as a validated judgment of playability (Validation).

Design for Environment and GRANTA eco-audit

Inputs and assumptions. GRANTA EduPack 2026 R1 model of the MVP: manufactured and used in North America, 3-year life, 4.7 lb of modeled parts (6061 frame, ABS case, PC window, motor, board, display, battery, charger). Where the impact is. By material energy, the main circuit assembly is 33.6% and the display 19.3%, though they weigh 0.27 lb together; the battery is 7.6%, the Top Plate and Baseplate 14%, the ABS case 5%. Within manufacturing, rolling the plate stock and molding the case are the largest loads. Toxins and hazards: lead-free solder on the board, battery chemistry, machining fluids and aluminium refining upstream.

Data quality. Board, display and battery use generic EduPack records ("printed wiring board, unspecified"; "LCD, unmounted"; Li-ion), so their numbers are order-of-magnitude. The model uses a Li-ion pack while the MVP uses a 9 V NiMH cell, and it still includes the old stop collar (0.01 lb); both change the totals by well under the 20% the report treats as significant. An earlier DfE worksheet run gave 582 MJ and 36.5 kg; the report above is the later model.

Not the lowest-impact option. A case-less bench unit or a cheaper display would lower impact, but the case is the safety enclosure and the readable display is an accessibility requirement; we kept both and target the display for cost-down. The modeled mass (2.15 kg in CAD plus electronics) also misses the 2 kg portability target.

Updated impact conclusions

  • Most consequential benefit: evidence-based decisions about when to retire a ball, keeping good balls in play longer and reducing replacement cost and waste for players and clubs.
  • Most significant harm: a wrong grade trusted too much (waste, disputes, lost trust), and a pinch injury if the interlock fails or is bypassed.
  • Most affected stakeholders: frequent players and clubs; older players and players with disabilities; supplier and recycling workers.
  • Most important design changes: door interlock, CHECK BALL state, icon + word + sound, no data collection, screw-fastened modules.
  • Remaining uncertainties: stiffness-to-playability agreement; interlock reliability; accessibility in real use; supplier practices; real recycling access; tests per charge on the NiMH battery.
  • Actions: blind grading, interlock tests on the first MVP build, the player study with varied users, supplier screening and a take-back plan.
  • Values for the business: measured accuracy before any performance claim, safety built into the hardware, access for players of all abilities, and a long-lasting, repairable product.
