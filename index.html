ME 455 · Gate Review 1 · Team 1

Nobody should lose a point to a soft ball.

A pickleball goes soft with play long before most players can feel it, and play suffers until someone notices. This portfolio documents the customer, market and competitive evidence behind CuPickle, a portable tester that lets a frequent, competitive player check at the court, before play or between games, whether a ball still plays like a good ball.

Gate Review 1 · Product Definition and Market Analysis

Introduction

52.8% rank consistent performance as their #1 purchase criterion Survey, n=36
46.7% say the hardest part is that damage is difficult to see Survey, n=15 asked

CuPickle starts from a nuisance every regular player knows: a ball that looked fine plays dead, and the point is gone before anyone notices. This page is a stand-alone overview of the problem, the customer, the evidence and the product direction; the pages linked below give the full design brief.

Project and product name

Project: Pickleball Ball-Condition Tester (tentative). Product name: CuPickle, “Keep the playable balls. Retire the non-playable ones.” Team CuPickle.

Sector and use context

The product is a sports-equipment accessory for pickleball. It is used by the player courtside, before play or between games, in open play, leagues and tournaments. Most respondents play outdoors (58.3%), on courts that often have no wall outlet, so the product must run on its own rechargeable battery.

The observed nuisance and today's workaround

Pickleballs soften gradually and without warning. A ball softens and develops soft spots with every hit: in Pickleball Science's measured play tests, a Franklin X-40 lost about 10% of its stiffness per hour of competitive play. Players usually find out mid-rally, when the ball bounces in a way nobody expected, and under USA Pickleball Rule 10.G a damaged ball is replaced only if every player agrees. The workaround is to look at, compress, bounce or hit the ball. That check takes about three seconds, but 52.8% of survey respondents rate their confidence in judging equipment at 3 or lower of 7.

Target customer and user

The primary customer and user is the individual frequent, competitive player: someone who plays at least weekly in league, rated or tournament play, brings their own balls, and checks them courtside. Half of respondents bring their own balls at least some of the time, and 64.7% of frequent players find an objective test useful, against 47.4% of occasional players. Clubs, venues and tournament directors that supply shared balls are a possible later segment, but that is an unvalidated hypothesis: the one facility owner interviewed saw no need.

Motivation

Competition is the top reason respondents play (48.5% rank it first), and consistent performance is their top equipment criterion (52.8%). A ball that has quietly gone soft attacks exactly what these players value: a fair, uninterrupted game they can trust.

Supporting evidence, in brief

The evidence comes from a 40-response survey (36 on the final form), four interviews (three players and one owner of two indoor venues), a customer journey map, published ball-degradation tests, a QFD, competitive benchmarking, a patent and standards search, and national market data. Soft balls are common (36.1% have played with one) and hard to spot (46.7% of the 15 asked), confidence in the manual check is low, and interest in a test is highest among frequent players. The SFIA 2026 Pickleball Single Sport Report counts 24.3 million U.S. players in 2025, 7.5 million of them CORE players who play eight or more times a year. The survey sample is young and university-dominated, so willingness to pay among older league and tournament players is the main open question.

Proposed product direction

A portable ball-condition tester that gives the player a keep-or-retire result tied to how the ball plays, judged against the USA Pickleball Equipment Standards Manual bounce standard (30–34 in. rebound from a 78 in. drop). It must work courtside on its own rechargeable battery, take 30 seconds or less per ball, need no special skill, not damage the ball, and make no claim of official certification. Gate Review 1 deliberately does not choose a sensing method.

Market value proposition

For frequent, competitive players whose balls go soft before they can tell, CuPickle offers earlier detection of hidden softening and more confidence in the ball than a manual check, in a form small enough to carry to the court. The instruments that measure equipment today are bench-bound and built for manufacturers and labs, costing $2,900 to $4,995; no commercial tester checks a ball in service. CuPickle targets a result in 30 seconds or less per ball at $100 or less. The manual check is faster, but players do not trust it, and because the check happens before play or between games they can trade a little time for a result they trust. Success will be measured by agreement with the USA Pickleball bounce test, time per ball, the gain in players' confidence, and willingness to buy at the target price.

Product goal

The goal of this project is to design a pickleball condition tester for frequent, competitive players that tells them objectively whether a ball still plays like a good ball before or between games so that they do not use a ball that reduces their performance.

The Gate Review 1 recommendation is proceed with revisions: the customer problem is well supported, while willingness to pay and the gain in confidence still need to be tested (Market Value Proposition).
