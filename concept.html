Gate Review 2 · Concept & Prototyping

Proposed Concept Description

A pickleball goes soft long before it cracks, and players cannot feel the difference until it costs them a point. DinkCheck squeezes the ball at several points around its circumference, measures how stiff it is and how much energy it loses, and tells the player whether it still plays like a good ball.

Figure 1. DinkCheck: concept render with the door closed and open (team, AI-assisted), and the full-scale alpha prototype of its press and roller cradle.

The opportunity from Gate Review 1

Gate Review 1 found that frequent, competitive players cannot reliably tell when a ball is worn out. Consistent performance is their top equipment criterion (52.8% rank it first), yet 52.8% rate their confidence in judging equipment at 3 or lower out of 7 [1]. The damage is mostly hidden: in Pickleball Science's play test, a Franklin X-40 lost about 10% of its stiffness per hour of play, falling from 93.5 to 75.2 lb/in over two hours, well before it cracked [2]. No commercial tester checks balls in service; the instruments that exist test paddles or factory quality and cost $2,900 to $4,995 (Competition Analysis). For Gate Review 2 the team narrowed the customer to the individual competitive player who carries the device to the court, rather than a station installed at a club.

How DinkCheck works

The player opens the door with the finger recess, sets the ball on a two-roller cradle, closes the door and presses the start button on top: two actions. A stepper-driven lead screw lowers a plate onto the ball; the load cell detects contact, then the plate compresses the ball a fixed distance while force is recorded on the way down and on the way back up. That gives two damage signals, stiffness (force ÷ deflection) and hysteresis (the loop between loading and unloading). The plate lifts, the rollers turn the ball, and the squeeze repeats at 4–6 positions. A position that reads far from the others flags a soft spot, or a ball resting on a hole or seam. The controller compares the results with a new ball of the same model and shows Playable or Non-playable on the readout, while the player watches the test through the door's window.

Fixed-displacement compression is the principle behind the compression test in the USA Pickleball Equipment Standards Manual, which requires new balls to average below 43 lbf under ASTM F1888 [4]. DinkCheck applies it to balls already in play.

Subsystems and how they interact

Housing

Dark upper shell for the drive, white lower shell for the cradle, windowed door and finger-pull recess.

Ball handling

Two silicone-sleeved rollers that cradle the ball and turn it between squeezes.

Compression

Stepper and lead screw drive the plate; a collar sets a 5 mm hard stop.

Force sensing

A load cell in the full force path reads force through loading and unloading.

Control

Computes stiffness, hysteresis and the outlier check, subtracts frame flex from a steel-ball calibration, grades the ball.

User interface

One start button and a readout on the top face; the window shows the test.

The controller commands the compression drive, the drive deforms the ball, the load cell returns the force, and the controller decides whether to squeeze again, roll the ball, or report a grade. The architecture drawing and operating sequence are on Proposed Concept & Alpha Prototype.

Benefits and differentiation

DinkCheck replaces a squeeze or bounce by feel with a measured answer. It detects softening before cracks are visible, and because it reads two signals at several positions it finds local soft spots that a single squeeze or bounce misses; Pickleball Science stopped its play test after three hours precisely because stiffness began to vary with ball orientation [2]. The player can watch the test happen, which builds trust, and it is the first tester aimed at balls in play rather than at paddles or factory quality control.

Why this concept was selected

Five meaningfully different concepts were compared against the test-gun datum with a Pugh matrix and a weighted matrix built from the GR2 QFD. Concept A, the Rolling Compression Station, was refined with two features from the CubeClamp (E), a hysteresis signal and steel-ball calibration, and the refined A′ scored highest at 3.26, ahead of the CubeClamp at 3.19 [5]. Because that gap is smaller than one rating step, the team decided on buildability: the CubeClamp's ball does not fit through its 40 mm opening, and it needs two synchronized motors and a machined flexure, while A′ uses off-the-shelf parts and two user actions. The full argument is on Downselection.

Advantages, limitations, risks and open questions

Advantages

  • Objective and repeatable: the motor, not the user, sets the squeeze.
  • Two damage signals at 4–6 positions per ball.
  • Non-destructive: a software force limit and a 5 mm hard stop cap the deformation; the door keeps fingers out of the squeeze.
  • Off-the-shelf parts, about $130 against the $400 prototype budget.

Limitations and risks

  • The alpha prototype showed that two plain rollers let the ball drift on the other axis, so the cradle needs multi-axial contact to control which spots are pressed.
  • At about 8 in. tall it is slightly too big for a bag; it exceeds the 2.5 L / 2 kg targets.
  • Two motors make the $33 unit-cost target hard.
  • One rotation axis tests about 30% of the surface.

Open questions. What Playable threshold fits each common ball model, and how should temperature be handled, given that stiffness changes strongly with temperature [6]?

Gate Review 2 recommendation

Proceed with DinkCheck (refined concept A′) into detailed design

First steps: a motorized prototype with a multi-axial cradle; drop tests of 20 new and used balls to confirm at least 90% agreement with the bounce test; 50 tests on control balls to confirm at most 0.5 in. of rebound change; and a smaller frame. If agreement falls short, the CubeClamp's curve-based approach is the fallback. Details: Proposed Concept & Alpha Prototype.
