Gate Review 1 · Customer & Market Research

Customers & Benefits

Frequent, competitive players lose points to balls that have gone soft or cracked before anyone can tell. This page describes who they are, where the problem happens in their session, and what a product would have to give them to be worth carrying to the court.

Survey n=36 Interviews n=4 Journey map QFD Rules & standards

The problem, from the court

A competitive player arrives for a league night or a tournament expecting to think about strategy, not equipment. Before the first game they pick a ball from their bag, bounce it and squeeze it. The check takes about three seconds (P3, a tournament player) [2], and most players do not trust it: 52.8% of survey respondents rate their confidence at 3 or lower out of 7 (n=36) [1]. Then, mid-game, a ball that looked fine skids or bounces low, and the rally is decided by the ball rather than the players. Nearly three in four respondents (72.2%, n=36) have played with a cracked ball [1], and the frustration named most often is that damage is difficult to see (46.7% of the 15 respondents who received that question) [1].

The damage is hidden for a physical reason. In Pickleball Science's play test of Franklin X-40 balls, stiffness fell about 10% per hour of competitive play, and by three hours the balls had soft spots, all before any crack appeared [14]. A player therefore keeps using a ball that already plays differently and only retires it once the damage is obvious.

This matters most to the players who care most about the game. Competition is the top reason respondents play (48.5% rank it first, n=33), and consistent performance is their top equipment criterion (52.8%, n=36) [1]. An unpredictable ball undermines both. The rest of this page therefore treats the benefit as earlier detection of hidden softening, leading to consistent play and confidence in the ball, rather than damage detection for its own sake. Interview participants are identified as P1–P4; their roles, the survey sample and the reasons some questions have fewer than 36 answers are set out under Evidence base and sample limits at the end of the page.

Customers, users and stakeholders

The primary customer and user is the individual frequent, competitive player: someone who plays at least weekly, takes part in league, rated or tournament play, brings their own balls, and checks them personally at the court before play or between games. This player both feels the failure and buys the balls, so user and purchaser are the same person. The survey supports the frequency and ownership parts of this definition: 47.2% play weekly or more (n=36), half bring their own balls at least some of the time (n=36), and frequent players are the most interested in an objective test (64.7% of the 17 frequent players against 47.4% of the 19 occasional players) [1]. The survey did not ask whether respondents play in leagues, rated events or tournaments, so the competitive part rests on competition being the top reason to play (48.5%, n=33) [1] and on P3, a ranked tournament player, who expected “diehard pickleballers” to buy one [2] Interpretation.

Facilities (clubs, indoor venues and tournament directors) are a possible secondary segment and an unvalidated hypothesis. They supply and retire shared balls, and the casual players we interviewed preferred that route:

“I don't think I would purchase something to determine whether or not it's good… when [P2] goes and plays at the club, they give her the balls.”P1, recreational player [2]
“I don't know that I would buy a device for that, but maybe if it was something at the club where you could just like throw the balls in and it would shoot out the ones that were good… That could be kind of cool.”P2, frequent player and facility co-owner [2]

The one facility owner we interviewed challenges that case directly. P4's venues do not inspect balls, because “it's pretty obvious when a ball has had it”; broken balls are thrown away, and “equipment is kind of the least of my problems” [2]. Facilities remain a possible secondary purchaser because they own shared inventory, but their need is unproven, and unattended screening of a venue's ball supply would require a different product. The product is therefore defined for the individual player's personal use at the court. Figure 1 and Table 1 set out each role.

Primary user profile

The survey describes the player the team could reach rather than the full target segment. Respondents skew young and university-affiliated: 80.6% are 18–24 (n=36). Most have played for one to three years (69.7%) or longer (15.2%) (n=33), 47.2% play weekly or more (n=36), and 58.3% play mainly outdoors (Figure 2) [1]. The survey did not ask occupation, income or family status; the age skew and university distribution suggest most respondents are students Interpretation. The interviews broaden the picture with an elementary-school teacher who plays with family (P1), a retired frequent player (P2), and a young, formerly college-competitive player who enters ranked tournaments (P3). P4 describes his venues' users as aged “8 to 80,” from first-timers to rated players who play “five times a week” [2]. Nationally, 24.3 million Americans played in 2025, 7.5 million of them CORE players (eight or more times a year), and ages 13–24 have the highest participation rate of any age group, according to the SFIA 2026 Pickleball Single Sport Report [8]. The young sample is therefore a real part of the market, but the target segment also includes older league and tournament regulars, such as P4's rated players, whom the survey under-represents.

Persona. The primary user is a composite of the modal respondent and the frequent-player subgroup: Maya, 21, a university student who plays four times a week, outdoors, in sessions of up to two hours, who plays mainly to compete and brings her own balls about half the time Composite. Maya stands for the part of the segment the survey reached; P3's tournament habits and P4's rated regulars show the same needs in older and more formally competitive players. Use environment and customer journey

The product will be used where the player plays, and for most respondents that is outdoors (58.3%, n=36) [1]: dedicated or shared tennis courts exposed to sun, cold and rough surfaces, with no storage and usually no wall outlet (Figure 5). Anything the player uses there has to fit in a bag and run on battery power. Breakage depends on level and ball: P2 sees a cracked ball “probably every other time I play,” and P3 goes through about one ball per session at a high level [2].

Indoor venues add a second setting. P4 describes former warehouses and big-box stores kept at a constant 65°F and run without staff for up to about 21 hours a day. A four-court facility “could easily go through 200, 300 balls a month,” balls “crack more quickly if it's cold,” and rough concrete or asphalt wears them faster [2]. These conditions shape how fast balls fail for every player. They would matter for a facility product only if the secondary segment is validated; the product defined here is carried and used by the player.

Journey map

The team's journey map follows one session from the player's point of view [3]. Target customer: a frequent, competitive player. Goal: play a tournament or league session without a ball failure deciding a point. Context: a league, club or tournament court, indoors or outdoors, with balls from the player's own bag or supplied by the club. Beginning and end: the journey starts when the player arrives to play and ends with the post-game review of what failed (Figure 8).

The map shows that the problem is felt in one place and solvable in another. The failure happens mid-rally, where nothing can be done without stopping play, so the product has to act earlier, at the moment the player picks a ball. The touchpoints and emotions around that moment set the conditions the product must meet.

Touchpoints. Products: the ball, the player's own bag of balls and, at clubs, the ball caddy. People: partner, opponents and, in sanctioned play, the referee, who must agree to or rule on a swap (Rules 10.G, 20.F) [6]. Information: the rulebook and word of mouth. Environment: court surface and temperature, which change how fast balls fail [2]. Services: club-supplied balls for open play, court booking, and often no staff on site [2].

Why the customer feels this way. The positive start reflects that players expect not to think about equipment. The neutral selection step reflects low confidence: 52.8% rate their judgment at 3 or below of 7 (n=36) [1]. The negative mid-game step is the failure itself: 72.2% have played with a cracked ball (n=36) [1]. The recovery afterwards reflects how little one ball costs: “if I lose 10 balls a day, I don't really care” (P4) [2]. That recovery is why a product cannot sell on saving balls; it has to sell on the game that was lost to a bad one.

From journey to customer needs. Each failure point becomes one of the customer needs on the Product Definition page. The hidden softening at ball selection becomes Identifies low-performance balls, measured as agreement with the USA Pickleball drop test. The low confidence at the same step becomes Increases confidence in quality, measured as the gain in users' self-rated confidence. Fitting the check into the time before play or between games becomes Tests quickly, measured as time to test a ball [4]. Targets for each are in the product characteristics and targets.

Priority customer benefits

The evidence points to one value story. Balls soften and crack before players can tell, and play suffers until the damage is obvious. A product that detects hidden softening earlier, gives the player more confidence than the hand check, and does so in a portable form at the court would give competitive players what they already buy equipment for: a ball that plays consistently. Why a 30-second check is acceptable when the hand check takes 3 seconds

Today's check is fast. P3 can usually tell “within 3 seconds” [2]. Speed is not the problem; trust is: 52.8% of respondents rate their confidence in that check at 3 or below of 7 (n=36) [1], and it cannot detect softening that has not yet cracked the ball [14]. The target is therefore 30 s or less per ball (Product Definition) [4]. Because the check happens before play or between games rather than mid-rally, a player can trade a little time for a result they trust Interpretation. Whether players accept that trade, and at what point extra seconds outweigh extra confidence, is to be tested in user evaluation.

The benefit is consistent play, not damage detection

Only 2.8% rank easy damage identification first (n=36) [1]. P3 cautioned that “life left in a ball is subjective”: it depends on who is playing, and professionals mostly use new balls [2]. A useful reading must connect to how the ball plays, such as bounce against the published standard [5], rather than to cosmetic wear. P3's own suggestion, a ball that “changes color when it cracks,” shows that a simple signal would be welcome [2].

Hypotheses still to validate

The customer definition above rests on customer reports. Before Gate Review 2 it needs targeted research with the competitive segment and objective performance evidence, organized around four hypotheses.

  • We hypothesize that frequent, competitive players across age groups (weekly or more; league, rated or tournament play) will pay about $100 for their own courtside ball check because a soft ball costs them points. This can be validated by a survey with a willingness-to-pay question, distributed through leagues, rated events and tournaments rather than personal networks.
  • We hypothesize that players misjudge balls that have softened but not cracked. This can be validated by drop-testing used balls against the 78 in./30–34 in. reference [5] and comparing the results with players' own keep-or-retire judgments.
  • We hypothesize that players will accept up to 30 s per ball before play or between games in exchange for a result they trust more than the hand check. This can be validated by user evaluation of a prototype that records time per test and self-rated confidence before and after.
  • We hypothesize that club and tournament operators running rated or competitive programs may form a secondary segment because consistent balls matter more to those members. This can be validated by interviews with more operators and tournament directors. (P4 runs mixed programs and saw no need.)

Evidence base and sample limits

This page draws on a consumer survey (36 responses to the final form plus 4 pilot responses) [1], four semi-structured interviews [2], the team's customer journey map [3], the team QFD [4], and published rules, standards and literature.
