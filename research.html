Gate Review 1 · Customer & Market Research

Market Research

What we asked, how we asked it, who answered, and what the evidence means for the customer, the opportunity and the product.

Survey n=40 Interviews n=4 Journey map Market data Secondary research

Research approach

The team used six methods to understand the customer, use context, current alternatives and market opportunity: a consumer survey, semi-structured interviews, customer journey mapping, product and competitor research (reported on the Competition page), secondary market data, and secondary research on how balls degrade. Two questions guided the work:

  1. How does the condition of a pickleball indirectly affect player performance, especially at higher levels of play?
  2. How could better evaluation of pickleballs help ensure that consistent-quality balls are used in club and tournament play?

A third question emerged during the research: who experiences the problem, and who would buy a solution? The player using a ball is often not the person who purchases, supplies or retires it.

Method 1: Survey

Purpose and research questions

  • How frequently do players experience damaged or degraded pickleballs?
  • What types of ball damage do players experience?
  • How do players currently decide whether a ball is still suitable for play, and how confident are they?
  • Which equipment characteristics do players value most?

Design, distribution and respondents

The survey was built in Google Forms and took about 3–5 minutes. It was piloted with four respondents and then revised: demographics were added (age, where respondents play, who provides the balls), together with a confidence question, a ranked-priority question, a frustrations question, a question on how useful an objective test would be, and a current-methods section. Non-players were excluded, and the “not applicable” option for session length was removed. Questions added after launch reached fewer people, so each result below reports its own n.

Survey link: Analytical Product Design survey (Google Form). The complete instrument is embedded in the appendix.

Recruitment. The form was released on Sep. 22, 2026 and remains open. David Shen shared it by text message and GroupMe with the University of Michigan pickleball club, the BYX fraternity and his friends, and other team members sent it to friends and family, who were asked to pass it on to people they knew who play pickleball. Snowball recruitment through personal networks favors people like the team: university-affiliated, 18–24, and recreational rather than club or tournament players. This explains the sample's age skew and means older and institutional users are under-represented.

Results

Competition is the top reason for playing (48.5% rank it first, n=33). Most respondents play outdoors (58.3%), and half provide their own balls at least some of the time; the rest rely on other players, clubs, leagues or tournaments (n=36). Of those asked about frustrations (n=15), 46.7% say damage is hard to see and 33.3% are unsure whether a change in play is the ball or themselves. Overall, 55.6% find an objective test useful (n=36) [1].

What the survey means for the product

  1. The benefit is consistent performance, not damage detection. Cracking is common, but players also report softness, warping, inconsistent bounce and changes in flight, and they rank consistency first (52.8%) while ranking easy damage identification last (2.8%). The product's result therefore has to describe how the ball will play, not only whether it is broken.
  2. The strongest user is the frequent player, not the novice. Frequent players are more confident (58.8% against 26.3%) and more interested in a test (64.7% against 47.4%), so the product should confirm an experienced player's judgment rather than replace it.
  3. There are two possible purchasers. Half of respondents supply their own balls, so frequent players can buy for themselves; clubs, leagues and tournaments supply balls in organized play and are a second purchaser. Which one to design for first is resolved on the Market Value Proposition page.
Limitations and bias

The sample is small and skews toward 18–24-year-old, university-affiliated respondents recruited through personal networks. Older players, highly competitive players and facility staff are under-represented. Questions added after launch have smaller bases (n=15–18). Several team members play pickleball and have experienced equipment problems themselves, which may have shaped how the problem, the hypotheses and the question wording were framed. One answer option (“Checking equipment takes too much time or effort”) assumes that checking is a burden and is not neutral, so results for that option are interpreted with caution. The survey measured perceived usefulness, not willingness to pay.

Method 2: Interviews

Purpose and research questions

  • How do players notice that a pickleball is starting to wear out or perform differently?
  • What makes a player consider a ball “good” or “bad”?
  • What do players do when they suspect something is wrong?
  • How important is ball condition in recreational versus competitive play?
  • Does increased durability still provide value if the ball plays differently?
  • Is an individual player or a club or facility the more realistic customer?

Semi-structured interviews let participants describe specific past experiences instead of choosing from fixed answers. The guide moved from background and equipment use, to the last time a ball did not play as expected, to how they check balls, replacement decisions, frustrations and desired outcomes. The guide opens with an introduction, a statement that participation is voluntary and can stop at any time, and a request for permission to record; P2's transcript records her permission. Later interviews will use a more formal recording request and open-ended wording (for example, “How, if at all, do you check a ball before playing?” in place of “Do you ever bounce it, squeeze it…?”).

Findings

1. Players judge a ball by how it plays, not only by visible cracks. Participants used bounce, feel off the paddle, sound and shape.

“Like when you bounce it, you can see if it bounces funny or if it just kind of dies on you. Like then I'll look at it to see if it's cracked.”P2 [2]
“If it doesn't bounce high, then, you know, it's not going to be a good, you know, a game to play.”P1 [2]

2. More durable does not mean better. The crack-resistant Lifetime ball “plays a little different” (P2); P4 says it is harder with “a lot more pop,” and that stronger players prefer it. The goal is not maximum ball life but how long a ball keeps performing within an acceptable range.

3. How serious the problem is depends on context. P2 sees a cracked ball “probably every other time I play” but calls it minor in social play; “if you're playing in a tournament and the ball breaks in the last point, that could be annoying.” P3 goes through about one ball per session at a high level.

4. Who buys depends on how seriously someone plays. The casual players (P1, P2) would not buy a personal tester and preferred a club-side sorter. The competitive player (P3) would not buy one at her current level, but expected “diehard pickleballers” would (see Customers).

5. The facility owner does not see ball inspection as a need.

“It's pretty obvious when a ball has had it, right? Usually, it starts like the spider cracks and then it just completely falls apart. So if a ball breaks, obviously, we throw it away.”P4 [2]

P4's four-court venue “could easily go through 200, 300 balls a month” at about $1.62 a ball; his main problems are building upkeep and programming, and “equipment is kind of the least of my problems.” This challenges the assumption that facilities urgently need a testing system, and supports treating facilities as a second purchaser behind frequent, competitive players. Whether either group will pay is not yet proven.

How the interviews tested our assumptions

  • Supported: balls crack, soften, lose bounce and become misshapen; players care about consistent bounce and familiar feel.
  • Revised: damage is not a major frustration for every player; it matters most in frequent or competitive play. A durable ball is only valuable if it still plays the same.
  • Revised: purchase interest rises with how seriously someone plays: casual players prefer a club-provided tester, competitive players may buy their own, and the one facility owner saw no need (P4).
  • Not supported: that players frequently throw away good balls, or that casual players want to own a personal tester.
Limitations

Four interviews cannot represent the market, and all participants came through personal networks. Some questions introduced the idea of a testing device directly, which may have primed answers, and several questions offered example answers (e.g. “bounce it, squeeze it…”) that may have steered responses. Only one facility operator has been interviewed.

Method 3: Customer journey mapping

The team mapped a frequent player's tournament session in three stages (getting ready, playing, post-game) and six touchpoints, recording activities, emotions and pain points [3]. The map located the product's opportunity at ball selection and post-game evaluation, since the mid-game failure is where the pain is felt but is too late to act on. The full map, with its target customer, goal, context, start and end, and evidence for each emotion, is on the Customers page.

Method 4: Market data

SFIA reports 24.3 million U.S. pickleball participants in 2025, up from 4.2 million in 2020. CORE players, who play eight or more times a year, grew from 1.4 million to 7.5 million over the same period, and ages 13–24 have the highest participation rate of any age segment [4]. USA Pickleball's location database lists 18,258 places to play with 82,613 courts [5]. The growth of frequent play means more balls in use across clubs, open play, leagues, tournaments and training. This does not prove demand for our product, but it supports pursuing an opportunity in high-use environments, and it places our young survey sample within a large, real segment.

Damage players cannot see

Measured softening. Pickleball Science played Franklin X-40 balls in competitive games among 3.5-level players and measured stiffness after each hour of play (about four games). Stiffness fell from 93.5 lb/in new to 84.0 lb/in after one hour and 75.2 lb/in after two, about 10% per hour [S15]. After three hours (about 12 games), readings were no longer repeatable because stiffness depended on the ball's orientation; the balls had soft spots that could be pressed in by finger [S15].

Useful life versus cracking. Pickleball Science estimated the X-40's usable life at no more than 4–8 games for tournament play and up to 12 for competitive play, yet the same ball was used for at least a dozen more recreational games before cracking [S15]; in a follow-up test it lasted about 30 games before cracking [S16]. Team interpretation: for more than half its life, the ball plays measurably worse without visible damage.

Bounce loss is not simply stiffness loss. As polymer chains break, internal damping increases, absorbing energy and lowering the bounce [S15]. A bounce test and a stiffness test therefore measure related but different properties. Models differ greatly: the Dura Fast 40's stiffness changed only 8% over 32 games, whereas the X-40 lost about 2.5% per game, ten times faster [S16]. A worn ball must be hit harder to reach the same speed, and a hard or soft spot can send a shot long or into the net [S15].

Relevant standards

The USA Pickleball Equipment Standards Manual requires an approved ball to bounce 30–34 in. (to the top of the ball) when dropped from 78 in. onto a granite surface plate, tested at 70 ± 5°F (rule 2.D.5) [S29]. Its compression requirement (rule 2.D.6) is that compressing the ball 0.25 in. under ASTM F1888-09 must take less than 43 lbf, a maximum stiffness of about 172 lb/in [S29][S20]. A new X-40 (93.5 lb/in) is about half as stiff as that limit [S20]. Hardness (40–50 Durometer D) is now recorded for reference only and is no longer a compliance requirement (rule 2.D.7) [S29].

Secondary sources disagree with the manual

A secondary source [S19] gives the bounce-test temperature as 75–80°F; the current manual (Rev. 3.0, Jan. 2025) specifies 70 ± 5°F. Some summaries also state the compression limit as “no more than 0.25 in. of displacement under a 43 lb load,” which reverses the test: ASTM F1888 measures the force needed to compress the ball 0.25 in., and the manual caps that force at 43 lbf. This portfolio uses the manual's values throughout.

What it means for the product

  • Supported: the survey finding that damage is hard to see; measured data show balls degrade well before they crack [S15][S16].
  • Revised: the product cannot rely on detecting cracks. It must detect gradual softening and soft spots, which a single bounce may miss [S15]. Accuracy against a reference test becomes a key characteristic and a technical risk (see the targets on Product Definition).
  • Added: degradation rates differ about tenfold between models [S16], strengthening the ball-compatibility requirement and suggesting model-specific thresholds.
  • Context: about 500 million balls a year and no repair option [S1][S8] make ball retirement a large, recurring decision. Recycling addresses disposal, not when to retire a ball.
Limitations

All measured degradation data come from Pickleball Science, a single independent tester using a manual force gauge on a small number of outdoor balls; the tester calls its compression test a first-order estimate [S15]. No indoor-ball degradation data were found. Waste figures disagree by about a factor of 100 and come mostly from recycling organizations, which have an interest in larger numbers. Several failure-mechanism sources are ball sellers' guides, so the team relied on mechanisms reported by more than one source.

Research synthesis

  • The target customer. Frequent, competitive players who already run informal ball checks and want them confirmed rather than replaced. They are the primary customer. Clubs, facilities and tournament directors hold shared inventory and are a second purchaser, though one owner saw no need.
  • What matters most. The nuisance is invisible degradation (46.7%), and measured data confirm it: a Franklin X-40 loses about 10% of its stiffness per hour of play and plays measurably worse for more than half its life before it cracks [S15][S16]. The tension is “is it the ball or me?” (33.3%). The aspiration is not thinking about equipment. The key touchpoints are ball selection and post-game evaluation.
  • What customers value. Consistent performance first (52.8%), then price, durability and long life. Easy damage identification is valued least (2.8%). A more durable ball that plays differently is not wanted.
  • Assumptions. Supported: damage is frequent and confidence is low. Revised: the product protects consistency rather than detecting damage; frequent, competitive players are the primary customer and facilities a second purchaser; interest rises with experience. Rejected: players want easier damage identification as such.
  • Implications for requirements. A result tied to ball performance (bounce against the standard); a result in 30 s or less, close to the seconds-long manual check; no damage to the ball; usable by a player courtside and unattended at a club; compatible with common balls, including the harder Lifetime ball.
  • What the product must detect. Gradual softening and soft spots, not just cracks; a single bounce may miss them [S15], and degradation rates differ about tenfold between ball models [S16].
  • Open questions. What will a club pay? How many balls does a competitive program retire, and at what cost? How does bounce decline with use? Do older and club-based players share these needs?

Complete final instrument (open in a new tab):

Pilot version and changes made after the pilot

Original (pilot) questions, as documented in Activity 10a [1]:

  1. How long have you been playing pickleball? (Less than 6 months; 6 months–1 year; 1–2 years; 2–3 years; 3+ years)
  2. How often do you play pickleball? (Regularly – several times a week; Often – about once a week; Sometimes – a few times a month; Rarely – less than once a month)
  3. How long do you play pickleball during one session? (Less than 30 min; 30 min–1 h; 1–2 h; 2–3 h; 3+ h)
  4. Rate your skill level (1 = never played; top = ranked player/professional)
  5. What is your favorite part about playing pickleball? (ranked: exercise, competition, teamwork, improving, other)
  6. In the time you have played pickleball, what have you personally experienced with any of your paddles?
  7. What brand do you use for your pickleball?
  8. What inconveniences you most when using a pickleball? (ranked)
  9. How often do you lose pickleballs during your games?
  10. Which types of pickleball damage have you personally experienced? (select all)
  11. How often do you encounter a damaged pickleball?
  12. How often do you set up a pickleball court?

Added after the pilot: age; where you primarily play; who provides the pickleballs; confidence in determining whether a pickleball is still in good condition (1–7); rank five characteristics when choosing equipment (consistent performance, price, durability, long use life, easy identification of damage); how useful an objective way to determine whether equipment is still performing properly would be (1–7); biggest frustration when deciding whether equipment is still suitable for play; and how you currently check a ball.
