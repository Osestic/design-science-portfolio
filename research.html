Section 02

Market Research

What we asked, why we asked it that way, what came back, and what we got wrong.

Survey n=40 Interviews Journey map

Method 1 — Survey

Research question and purpose

How often do players experience damaged or degraded equipment, what kinds of damage occur, and how do they currently decide whether a ball or paddle is still usable? A survey was appropriate because these are frequency and prevalence questions across a population, not questions about reasoning — we needed to know how common the nuisance is before investing in depth.

Assumptions we set out to test

  • Pickleballs degrade often enough to be a meaningful nuisance.
  • Performance changes before damage becomes visible.
  • Players are not always confident judging whether a ball is usable.
  • Players keep using degraded balls, or discard good ones, because they are unsure.
  • Durability, performance consistency and easy damage identification are important needs.
  • Clubs have more incentive than individuals to evaluate equipment condition.

Design, distribution and respondents

A ten-question Google Form, piloted with four respondents and then revised. The pilot lacked several questions added afterwards, so those questions report n=36 rather than the full n=40. Revisions after the pilot added demographics (who provides the balls, age, where they play), a confidence question, a ranked-priority section, a frustrations question, and a current-solutions section; the "not applicable" option on session length was removed to avoid inflating the shortest bucket, and non-players were excluded.

Results

What the survey means for the product

Damage is common and detection is hard, but "easy to identify damage" is the least valued purchase criterion. These are only contradictory if the product is sold as a damage detector. Sold as a way to protect consistent performance — the thing 52.8% rank first — the same capability addresses the dominant need. That reframing is the single most important product-definition consequence of the survey.

Limitations and bias

The sample is small (36) and skews heavily toward 18–24-year-old, University of Michigan–affiliated respondents, so it generalises poorly to the older recreational players who make up much of the sport. Several questions were added after the pilot and have smaller bases still (n=15 to n=18), which is why the frustration and full-ranking figures are reported with their own denominators. Most importantly, we never asked willingness to pay, and we never asked usefulness and willingness to pay in the same instrument — so the survey establishes interest, not demand.

Method 2 — Interviews

Purpose and participant selection

The survey could tell us how often equipment fails, but not how players reason about it. Interviews were used to understand how a player notices wear, what they do next, and whether the problem is worth solving for individuals or for institutions. Participants were regular players who had dealt with worn-out equipment; two were interviewed in depth, roughly 30 minutes each, recorded with consent under a semi-structured guide.

Structure

The guide moved from background and equipment use, to a critical-incident question (the last time a ball did not play the way they expected), to how they check equipment, to paddles, frustrations and workarounds, and finally to what they want. Asking for the most recent specific incident, rather than general opinion, was deliberate: it produces recall of what actually happened instead of a theory about what usually happens.

What we heard

  • The customer may not be the player. Both participants felt a condition-checking product made more sense for a club or facility than as something an individual recreational player would buy. This was the clearest contradiction of our starting assumption.
  • Feel changes before appearance does. Consistent with the survey finding that invisible damage is the leading frustration.
  • Checking is a habit, not a procedure. Bouncing, squeezing, comparing against another ball, and looking it over — performed constantly, trusted only partly.
  • Durability alone is not the answer. A longer-lasting ball is only attractive if it still plays like a normal ball, which rules out "just make a tougher ball" as a product direction.
Limitations

Two interviews is a small base for the strongest conclusion we drew from them — that clubs are the better customer. Both participants were recruited from the same community as the survey sample. The club/facility hypothesis is currently supported by two conversations and no institutional interview at all; no club manager, tournament organiser or facility buyer has been interviewed.

Research synthesis

Bringing the survey, the interviews and the journey map together:

  • About the customer. Frequent, competitive-minded players who already run informal equipment checks and want them validated — not replaced.
  • Which touchpoints matter. Pre-match selection and post-game evaluation. The mid-game failure is what hurts, but it is too late to act on; the product has to move the decision earlier.
  • What customers value. Consistent performance first, by a wide margin, then price and durability.
  • Assumption supported: degradation is frequent, invisible damage is the top frustration, and confidence is genuinely split.
  • Assumption revised: the buyer may be the club rather than the player, and interest rises with expertise rather than falling.
  • Assumption rejected: that players want easier damage identification as such. They rank it last. They want consistent performance, of which detection is a means.
  • Open questions. What would anyone pay? Would a club buy? How much effort will a player tolerate before a match? None of these are answered by the current evidence.
