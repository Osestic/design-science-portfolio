Gate Review 1 · Customer & Market Research

Market Research

Pickleballs lose much of their bounce and stiffness long before they crack, and the frequent, competitive players who care most about consistent play cannot reliably tell when that has happened. Six research methods, from a 36-person survey to published stiffness measurements and the official equipment standard, point to the same opportunity: a courtside check that catches hidden softening and gives players a result they trust.

Survey n=36 (+4 pilot) Interviews P1–P4 Journey map Market data Secondary research Standards

What the research shows

The evidence supports four conclusions, which the research synthesis develops in full. Each one draws on more than one method, and each one shapes the product definition.

  1. Balls play worse long before they look worse. In Pickleball Science's play test of the Franklin X-40, stiffness fell about 10% per hour of play, yet the ball did not crack until about 30 games [S15][S16]. Players notice the symptom: 72.2% have played with a cracked ball, and 46.7% say damage is hard to see (n=15) [1].
  2. Players want consistency but do not trust their own check. Consistent performance is the top equipment criterion (52.8%), yet 52.8% rate their confidence in judging a ball at 3 or below of 7 [1]. The manual check is fast, about 3 s for a tournament player (P3), but it relies on look, bounce and feel [2].
  3. The customer is the frequent, competitive player, and facility demand is unproven. Frequent players find an objective test more useful (64.7% against 47.4% of occasional players), and half of all respondents bring their own balls [1]. The one facility owner interviewed sees no need: “equipment is kind of the least of my problems” (P4) [2]. Facilities therefore remain a possible secondary segment, not the design target.
  4. The official standard gives the product a reference, but only once secondary claims are checked against it. The USA Pickleball Equipment Standards Manual requires a 30–34 in. rebound from 78 in. at 70 ± 5°F and a compression force below 43 lbf [S29]. Secondary summaries misstate both the test temperature and the direction of the compression test, so this portfolio uses the manual's values only.

One body of evidence is deliberately not used to justify the product: published estimates of ball waste disagree with each other by about a factor of 100 (Table 9), and most come from recycling organizations. The team treats waste as context for social impact, not as proof of demand.

Research approach

Two questions guided the work: how the condition of a pickleball affects player performance, especially at higher levels of play, and how better evaluation could keep consistent-quality balls in club and tournament play. A third question follows from the first two: who experiences the problem, and who would buy a solution? The player using a ball is often not the person who supplies or retires it, so each method below also tests who the customer is.

No single method could answer all three questions. The survey measures how common the problem is; interviews explain how players reason about it; the journey map locates where a product can act; market data sizes the population; secondary research measures what players cannot; and the equipment standard defines what a “good” ball is. Product and competitor research is reported on the Competition Analysis page.

Survey: players value consistency but cannot judge it

The survey shows that ball damage is a normal part of play, that players rank consistent performance far above every other equipment criterion, and that about half of them do not trust their own judgment of a ball. Interest in an objective test is highest among frequent players, which makes them the target customer.

Research questions and hypotheses

The survey asked how often players meet damaged or degraded balls, what kinds of damage they see, how they decide whether a ball is still playable and how confident they are, and which equipment characteristics they value most. A survey fits these questions because each one asks “how many?” rather than “why?”. Table 7 lists the hypotheses the team set out to test and what the data showed.

Design, distribution and respondents

The survey was built in Google Forms and takes about 3–5 minutes (the form's introduction promises under 8). Its introduction states that participation is voluntary and that answers are anonymized before being shared outside the five-person team. It was piloted with four respondents and then revised: demographics were added (age, where respondents play, who provides the balls), together with a confidence question, a ranked-priority question, a frustrations question, a question on how useful an objective test would be, and a current-methods section. Non-players were excluded, and the “not applicable” option for session length was removed. Every question, with its answer options, is transcribed in the appendix; the live form is the ME455 Pickleball Survey (Google Form).

Recruitment. The form was released on Sep. 22, 2026 and remains open. David Shen shared it by text message and GroupMe with the University of Michigan pickleball club, the BYX fraternity and his friends, and other team members sent it to friends and family, who were asked to pass it on to people they knew who play pickleball. Snowball recruitment through personal networks favors people like the team: university-affiliated, 18–24, and recreational rather than club or tournament players. This explains the sample's age skew and means older and institutional users are under-represented.

Customer segment definition. Results are reported for all respondents and for the frequent-player segment, defined as respondents who play “Regularly” (several times a week) or “Often” (about once a week), n=17. This weekly-or-more group is the closest match in the sample to the target customer, the frequent, competitive player.

Why some results have smaller denominators

The survey received 40 responses. The four pilot responses came from the pilot form, which lacked several questions, so all results on this site use the 36 final-form responses as the base. Some results have smaller bases for documented reasons. The questions on current checking methods (n=16) and frustrations (n=15) were added after launch and reached only later respondents. The ranking question records a first choice for all 36 respondents, but only 18 ranked all five characteristics, so the average rank uses n=18. The reason-for-playing ranking was answered by 33. Each figure and statement reports its own n, and results from the smaller bases are treated as indicative rather than conclusive.

Players rank consistent performance first and damage identification last

This result reframes the product. Players do not want a better way to spot damage for its own sake; they want every ball to play the same. A tester is valuable only if its result predicts how the ball will play, which is why the first customer need is Identifies low-performance balls rather than “finds cracks” (Product Definition).

Damage is a normal condition of play, not an edge case

Cracking is the damage players notice, but more than a third also report softening, the damage that is hardest to see. Interpretation Because softening is invisible from outside, the 36.1% figure is likely a lower bound: the secondary research shows that every ball tested softened measurably long before it cracked.

About half of players doubt their own judgment

More than half of all players rate their confidence at 3 or below. This is the gap a product can close, and it is why Increases confidence in quality is a customer need with its own measurable target: a gain on the same 7-point scale after using the product (Product Definition).

Every current check is fast but subjective

These manual checks are CuPickle's real competition. They are free and take seconds, and P3 can usually judge a ball “within 3 seconds” [2], but none of them detects the gradual softening documented below. A product therefore has to win on trust rather than speed. The team set the time target at 30 s or less per ball because the check happens before play or between games, where players will trade a little time for a result they trust.

Frequent players want a test most, even though they trust themselves more

Frequent players are both more confident and more interested in a test, so the product's role is to confirm an experienced player's judgment rather than replace it. This group also plays for the outcome: competition is the top reason for playing (48.5% rank it first, n=33), most respondents play mostly outdoors (58.3%), and half provide their own balls at least some of the time; the rest rely on other players, clubs, leagues or tournaments (n=36) [1]. Overall, 55.6% find an objective test useful (n=36).

Implications for the customer and product definition

  1. The benefit is consistent performance, not damage detection. Cracking is common, but players also report softness, warping, inconsistent bounce and changes in flight, and they rank consistency first (52.8%) while ranking easy damage identification last (2.8%). The product's result therefore has to describe how the ball will play, not only whether it is broken.
  2. The target customer is the frequent player, not the novice. Frequent players are more confident (58.8% against 26.3%) and more interested in a test (64.7% against 47.4%), so the product should confirm an experienced player's judgment rather than replace it.
  3. The player can be the purchaser. Half of respondents supply their own balls, so frequent players can buy and use the product for themselves at the court. Clubs, leagues and tournaments supply balls in organized play and are a possible secondary segment, but the survey did not measure their interest and the one operator interviewed saw no need (Interviews).
Limitations and bias

The sample is small and skews toward 18–24-year-old, university-affiliated respondents recruited through personal networks. Older players, highly competitive league, rated and tournament players, and facility staff are under-represented. Questions added after launch have smaller bases (n=15–18; see denominators). Several team members play pickleball and have experienced equipment problems themselves, which may have shaped how the problem, the hypotheses and the question wording were framed. One answer option (“Checking equipment takes too much time or effort”) assumes that checking is a burden and is not neutral, so results for that option are interpreted with caution. The survey measured perceived usefulness, not willingness to pay.

Interviews: ball condition matters to serious players, not to the facility owner

The interviews explain the survey's numbers. Players judge a ball by how it plays, the importance of that judgment rises with the level of play, and the one facility owner treats balls as a cheap consumable that needs no inspection. Together they point the product at the individual competitive player.

Purpose and research questions

  • How do players notice that a pickleball is starting to wear out or perform differently?
  • What makes a player consider a ball “good” or “bad”?
  • What do players do when they suspect something is wrong?
  • How important is ball condition in recreational versus competitive play?
  • Does increased durability still provide value if the ball plays differently?
  • Is an individual player or a club or facility the more realistic customer?

Structure and conduct

Semi-structured interviews let participants describe specific past experiences instead of choosing from fixed answers, which is what the “how” and “why” questions above require. The guide moved from background and equipment use, to the last time a ball did not play as expected, to how they check balls, replacement decisions, frustrations and desired outcomes. It opens with an introduction, a statement that participation is voluntary and can stop at any time, and a request for permission to record; P2's transcript records her permission. Rhia Gupta conducted P1, P2 and P4, which were recorded and transcribed (P2 and P4 by phone); David Shen interviewed P3 while Nicholas Henry took typed notes and asked follow-up questions (Table 8).

Themes and quotations

1. Players judge a ball by how it plays, not only by visible cracks. Participants used bounce, feel on contact, sound and shape, and turned to a visual check only after the ball misbehaved. P3 slaps the ball against the floor and can usually tell “within 3 seconds.”

“Like when you bounce it, you can see if it bounces funny or if it just kind of dies on you. Like then I'll look at it to see if it's cracked.”P2 [2]
“If it doesn't bounce high, then, you know, it's not going to be a good, you know, a game to play.”P1 [2]

2. More durable does not mean better. The crack-resistant Lifetime ball “plays a little different” (P2); P4 says it is harder with “a lot more pop,” and that stronger players prefer it. The goal is not maximum ball life but how long a ball keeps performing within an acceptable range, which is why longer-lasting balls are substitutes rather than a solution.

3. How serious the problem is depends on the level of play. P2 sees a cracked ball “probably every other time I play” but calls it minor in social play; “if you're playing in a tournament and the ball breaks in the last point, that could be annoying.” P3 goes through about one ball per session at a high level.

4. Willingness to buy rises with commitment. The casual players (P1, P2) would not buy a personal tester and preferred one provided at the club. The competitive player (P3) would not buy one at her current level, but expected “diehard pickleballers” would (see Customers). No participant was asked a price, so willingness to pay remains unmeasured.

5. The facility owner does not see ball inspection as a need.

“It's pretty obvious when a ball has had it, right? Usually, it starts like the spider cracks and then it just completely falls apart. So if a ball breaks, obviously, we throw it away.”P4 [2]

P4's four-court venue “could easily go through 200, 300 balls a month” at about $1.62 a ball; his main problems are building upkeep and programming, and “equipment is kind of the least of my problems.” This evidence challenges the assumption that facilities need a testing system. It is one operator, so it does not rule facilities out, but it is the main reason the product is defined for the individual player and facilities are kept as an unvalidated secondary segment.

What the interviews confirm, refine and contradict

  • Confirmed: balls crack, soften, lose bounce and become misshapen; players care about consistent bounce and familiar feel.
  • Refined: damage is not a major frustration for every player; it matters most in frequent or competitive play. A durable ball is only valuable if it still plays the same.
  • Refined: purchase interest rises with how seriously someone plays. Casual players would not own a tester, and competitive players may buy their own.
  • Contradicted: that facilities urgently need ball inspection (P4), that players frequently throw away good balls, or that casual players want to own a personal tester.
Limitations

Four interviews cannot represent the market, and all participants came through personal networks. Some questions introduced the idea of a testing device directly, which may have primed answers, and several offered example answers (e.g. “Do you ever bounce it, squeeze it…?”) that may have steered responses; next-phase interviews will use open wording such as “How, if at all, do you check a ball before playing?” Only one competitive player and one facility operator have been interviewed.

Journey mapping: the product must act before play, not mid-rally

The journey map shows that the pain is felt mid-rally, when a ball bounces wrong and decides a point, but that a product can only act before that moment: when the player picks a ball before play or between games. The post-game review, when broken balls are thrown out, is a second possible touchpoint, but the product is defined for the check before play.

Research question and fit. Where in a session does the ball problem occur, and where could a product intervene? Survey percentages and interview themes say what goes wrong; a journey map places it in time and place, which is what a product needs in order to fit the player's routine.

Design and subject. The team mapped a frequent, competitive player's tournament session in three stages (getting ready, playing, post-game) and six touchpoints, recording activities, emotions and pain points [3]. Each emotion is traced to survey or interview evidence: the neutral ball-selection step reflects low confidence (52.8% rate themselves 3 or below), and the negative mid-game step is the failure itself (72.2% have played with a cracked ball) [1]. The full map, with its target customer, goal, context and evidence for each step, is Figure 7 on the Customers & Benefits page.

What it means. Picking a ball is the one step where the player has both time and a reason to check, so the product is defined for personal use at the court, before play or between games. Players check balls one at a time from a bag, which makes time per ball and the number of steps (Tests quickly, Fits existing routine) real requirements rather than conveniences.

Limitations

The map is a composite built by the team from survey and interview evidence, not a record of one observed session, so its emotional arc is an interpretation. Observing league or tournament play would test it.

Market data: a large and fast-growing base of frequent players

Published participation data show that the population of frequent players is large and growing quickly, so the target segment is real even though our survey sample is small.

Research question and fit. Is the customer base large and engaged enough to justify a product? Industry participation counts answer this at a scale no student survey can.

Evidence. The SFIA 2026 Pickleball Single Sport Report counts 24.3 million U.S. pickleball participants in 2025, up from 4.2 million in 2020. CORE players, who play eight or more times a year, grew from 1.4 million to 7.5 million over the same period, and ages 13–24 have the highest participation rate of any age segment [4]. The USA Pickleball Annual Growth Report lists 18,258 places to play with 82,613 courts [5].

What it means. More frequent play means more balls in service and more ball-retirement decisions. The 13–24 participation finding places our young survey sample inside a large, real segment rather than at its edge. The Market Value Proposition page builds the market estimate from these counts.

Limitations

Participation is not demand for a tester. SFIA's CORE threshold (eight or more times a year) is broader than the weekly, competitive player the product targets, so the target segment is a subset of 7.5 million whose size is not yet measured. Other sources give different player totals (P3 Cares cites more than 36 million U.S. players [S1]), so the team uses SFIA, the industry's published survey, throughout.

Secondary research: balls degrade long before damage shows

Published measurements confirm what players struggle to sense: a ball loses stiffness steadily from its first game and plays measurably worse for more than half its life before it cracks. No repair exists, and today's workarounds address cracks, shape or disposal rather than hidden softening.

Research questions and fit. How many balls fail each year? Why do they fail? How much does a ball degrade before damage becomes visible? What do players and facilities do about worn balls today? Published sources were the right method because measured degradation data and industry waste figures cannot come from our survey or interviews. Rhia Gupta compiled the sources on Sep. 30, 2026; source numbers [S1]–[S28] follow her summary. Figures marked team calculation are the team's own interpretation.

Balls play measurably worse long before they crack

Measured softening. In “How Many Games Will a Pickleball Last?”, Pickleball Science played Franklin X-40 balls in competitive games among 3.5-level players and measured stiffness after each hour of play (about four games). Stiffness fell from 93.5 lb/in new to 84.0 lb/in after one hour and 75.2 lb/in after two, about 10% per hour [S15]. After three hours (about 12 games), readings were no longer repeatable because stiffness depended on the ball's orientation; the balls had soft spots that could be pressed in by finger [S15].

This is the core evidence for the opportunity. The longest part of a ball's life is spent in the shaded band, where it plays worse but looks fine, so a visual check cannot catch it. A product that detects softening in this band offers something no manual check can.

Useful life versus cracking. Pickleball Science estimated the X-40's usable life at no more than 4–8 games for tournament play and up to 12 for competitive play, yet the same ball was used for at least a dozen more recreational games before cracking [S15]; in a follow-up comparison it lasted about 30 games before cracking [S16]. Team interpretation: for more than half its life, the ball plays measurably worse without visible damage.

Bounce loss is not simply stiffness loss. As polymer chains break, internal damping increases, absorbing energy and lowering the bounce [S15]. A bounce test and a stiffness test therefore measure related but different properties. Models differ greatly: the Dura Fast 40's stiffness changed only 8% over 32 games, whereas the X-40 lost about 2.5% per game, ten times faster [S16]. A worn ball must be hit harder to reach the same speed, and a hard or soft spot can send a shot long or into the net [S15]. For the product, this means a single threshold will not fit every ball model, and a single measurement may miss a soft spot.

Implications for the product definition

  • Hidden damage is real. The survey finding that damage is hard to see is confirmed by measurement: balls degrade well before they crack [S15][S16].
  • Crack detection is not enough. The product must detect gradual softening and soft spots, which a single bounce may miss [S15]. Agreement with a reference test therefore becomes a key characteristic and a technical risk (see the targets on Product Definition).
  • One threshold will not fit every ball. Degradation rates differ about tenfold between models [S16], which strengthens the Works with any ball need and suggests model-specific thresholds.
  • Retirement is a recurring decision with no repair option. About 500 million balls a year (an industry estimate) and no repair [S1][S8] make the keep-or-retire decision frequent. Recycling addresses disposal, not when to retire a ball.
Limitations

All measured degradation data come from Pickleball Science, a single independent tester using a manual force gauge on a small number of outdoor balls; the tester calls its compression test a first-order estimate [S15]. No indoor-ball degradation data were found. Waste figures disagree by about a factor of 100 and come mostly from recycling organizations, which have an interest in larger numbers. Several failure-mechanism sources are ball sellers' guides, so the team relied on mechanisms reported by more than one source.

Standards review: the official tests define a good ball

The USA Pickleball standard gives the product an objective definition of a ball that plays correctly: a 30–34 in. rebound and a compression force below 43 lbf, both under controlled conditions. Several secondary summaries misstate these tests, so the team uses the manual itself.

Research question and fit. What measurable test defines a ball that still plays as intended? Player judgments vary, and the governing body's approval tests are the accepted reference for sanctioned play. The team read the USA Pickleball Equipment Standards Manual (Rev. 3.0, Jan. 2025) directly and checked each secondary summary against it.

Evidence. An approved ball must bounce 30–34 in. (to the top of the ball) when dropped from 78 in. onto a granite surface plate, tested at 70 ± 5°F (rule 2.D.5) [S29]. The compression requirement (rule 2.D.6) is that compressing the ball 0.25 in. under ASTM F1888-09 must take less than 43 lbf, a maximum stiffness of about 172 lb/in [S29][S20]. A new X-40 (93.5 lb/in) is about half as stiff as that limit [S20]. Hardness (40–50 Durometer D) is recorded for reference only and is not a compliance requirement (rule 2.D.7) [S29].

Secondary sources disagree with the manual

A secondary source [S19] gives the bounce-test temperature as 75–80°F; the current manual (Rev. 3.0, Jan. 2025) specifies 70 ± 5°F. Some summaries also state the compression limit as “no more than 0.25 in. of displacement under a 43 lb load,” which reverses the test: ASTM F1888 measures the force needed to compress the ball 0.25 in., and the manual caps that force at 43 lbf. This portfolio uses the manual's values throughout.

What it means. The bounce test gives the product a reference for its first need, Identifies low-performance balls: a ball that no longer rebounds 30–34 in. no longer plays like an approved ball. The compression test shows that stiffness can be measured with a fixed displacement, but its 43 lbf limit is an upper bound for new balls and says nothing about how soft a worn ball may become. The standard also fixes the test temperature, which matters because temperature alone changes stiffness [S13].

Limitations

The standard approves new ball models; it does not define when a ball in service should be retired. Using its bounce range as a retirement threshold is a team decision Assumption that next-phase testing must check against players' own judgments.

Research synthesis

Read together, the six methods support four bottom-line conclusions. Each conclusion below names the methods behind it and what it means for the product.

1. The problem is hidden softening, felt as inconsistent play

Players rank consistent performance first (52.8%) and say damage is hard to see (46.7%, n=15) [1]. Interviewees judge balls by bounce and feel, not cracks [2]. Published measurements explain why: an X-40 loses about 10% of its stiffness per hour and plays measurably worse for more than half its life before cracking [S15][S16]. Survey, interviews, secondary research.

2. The customer is the frequent, competitive player

Frequent players want a test most (64.7% against 47.4%) [1]; competitive play is where a bad ball costs a point (P2, P3) [2]; and 7.5 million CORE players form a large, growing pool [4]. Half of respondents bring their own balls, so this player can buy and use the product personally. Survey, interviews, market data.

3. The product must win on trust, at the moment a ball is chosen

The manual check takes about 3 s, yet 52.8% rate their confidence at 3 or below of 7 [1][2]. The journey map places the only useful intervention before play or between games, not mid-rally [3]. A result in 30 s or less is acceptable there because players trade a little time for a result they trust. Survey, interviews, journey map.

4. An objective reference exists, but facility demand and waste do not support the case

The USA Pickleball bounce standard (30–34 in. from 78 in.) gives a reference for “good” [S29]. The one facility owner interviewed sees no need (P4) [2], and waste estimates conflict by a factor of 100. Facilities remain an unvalidated secondary segment, and waste stays context. Standards, interviews, secondary research.

Open questions for the next phase

  • Willingness to pay. Neither the survey nor the interviews asked a price. A willingness-to-pay question aimed at league, rated and tournament players is the most important gap.
  • Do player judgments match measurements? A controlled comparison of players' keep-or-retire judgments against bounce and stiffness measurements of the same balls would test whether hidden softening changes how a ball plays for them, and where the retirement threshold should sit.
  • Is the segment broader than our sample? Targeted research with league, rated and tournament players across age groups, and with more tournament directors and club operators, would test whether the need holds beyond university-aged respondents and whether facilities are a viable secondary segment.
  • How does bounce decline with use? Published data cover stiffness of outdoor balls only; bounce loss and indoor-ball degradation have not been measured.
