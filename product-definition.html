The product

Our goal is to develop CuPickle, a pickleball condition tester that tells a player whether a ball still plays like a good ball, before or between games. It gives an objective check in place of visual inspection or a squeeze by feel. Surveys and interviews show that players care most about consistent performance and confidence in their equipment, and that their current checks are quick but subjective [1][2]. The opportunity is a test that detects the softening players cannot feel without taking long, being hard to use, or damaging the ball.

The customer and user is the individual frequent, competitive player who brings their own balls to open play, leagues and tournaments and carries the tester to the court (see Customers). The product succeeds if it agrees with the USA Pickleball reference bounce test, raises players' confidence in their balls, and does both quickly, repeatably and without harming the ball.

One reconciled set of eleven needs governs the project. They are written in the customer's words, each with a definition another team could apply consistently, and each is carried unchanged into the House of Quality and the downselection.

How stakeholders weight the needs

Four stakeholders rank the same eleven needs, and they disagree; the disagreements are where product decisions live. Each rank is converted to points (rank 1 = 11 points, rank 11 = 1), multiplied by the stakeholder's weight, summed, and normalized. The player carries 55% as the primary user and buyer; the producer 25%, because it decides whether the product can be built and sold at the target price; court owners and other players on court 10% each, because test time affects them but they neither buy nor build the product [3].

Requirements, objectives and constraints

Requirements

  • Agrees with the USA Pickleball bounce test on at least 90% of balls
  • Changes a ball's rebound by no more than 0.5 in. after 50 tests
  • Tests a ball in 30 s or less, in two user actions or fewer
  • Grades the five most-used ball models

Objectives

  • Raise player confidence by at least 2 points on a 7-point scale
  • Resolve depth changes of 0.025 mm
  • Retail at $100 or less, with a unit cost near $33 at volume
  • Pack into 2.5 L at 2 kg or less; last 1,500 test cycles

Constraints (producer)

  • Prototype manufacturing cost under $400 (course budget)
  • Manufacturable with course resources, in under 8 weeks
  • Completed this semester
  • No claim of USA Pickleball certification or approval [5]

House of Quality

The QFD relates the eleven needs to fifteen measurable specifications, with stakeholder ranks on the left, a signed correlation roof, targets and their origins below, and four ball-evaluation alternatives as benchmarks [3]. Relationship strength: 9 strong, 3 moderate, 1 weak. Benchmarks are rated 1–5 against each need (5 = best).

Reading the matrix

32 of the 165 need–specification pairs carry a relationship, 16 of them strong. Every need has at least one strong (9) relationship. The highest-value cell is identifies low-performance balls × agreement with the drop test: agreement is the operational definition of correctly identifying a worn ball, and resolution (S2) sets the ceiling on it, because degradation appears as a small change in deformation at a given force [7].

What the roof shows

43 of the 105 specification pairs carry a correlation. Three clusters emerge, each internally positive, with the design tension running between them.

  • Performance: agreement, resolution and confidence are tightly coupled (S1 × S2 and S1 × S5, 9+). Improving resolution improves accuracy, which builds confidence; this cluster creates customer value.
  • Convenience: operator speed, time to test and user actions move together (S3 × S7, S3 × S8, S7 × S8, 9+), so one architectural decision tends to set all three.
  • Cost: retail price, manufacturing cost, volume and weight are positively coupled (S6 × S13, S9 × S10, 9+), with durability the internal exception (S12 × S13, 9−).

Every strong negative crosses between clusters: accuracy against time (S1 × S7, 9−), the central design tension, and resolution against cost (S2 × S13, 9−). Compatibility (S11) is negative against accuracy, cost, weight and volume, because accepting the full ±1.25 mm diameter range makes the device larger, heavier and harder to calibrate; limiting scope to five ball models rather than all 393 approved models [6] is the decision that keeps the other targets reachable.

Planned validation

  • Blind grading of 20 new and used balls by squeeze, visual inspection, durometer and CuPickle against the 78 in. drop test, replacing the estimated accuracies (S1).
  • 50 test cycles on control balls with before-and-after drop tests (S4).
  • A before-and-after confidence survey with more than 20 players (S5).
  • Timed tests and action counts with first-time users (S3, S7, S8), and an aesthetics poll of 20 players (S14).

Main tradeoffs

Accuracy versus time. More measurement positions find local soft spots but add seconds (S1 × S7, 9−). CuPickle resolves this toward accuracy, testing 4–6 positions within the 30 s limit.

Resolution versus cost and size. Fine resolution demands a stiffer structure and a better sensor (S2 × S13, 9−), and a stiffer structure is bulkier and heavier, which works against the 2.5 L and 2 kg targets.

Compatibility versus everything. Accepting every ball's diameter and stiffness adds size, cost and calibration effort; supporting the five most-used models is a deliberate scope decision.
