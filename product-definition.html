The product

Our goal is to develop DinkCheck, a pickleball condition tester that helps players and clubs determine whether a ball is still in good playing condition before or during play. It gives players a practical, objective check instead of relying on visual inspection or personal intuition. Surveys and interviews show that players care most about performance consistency, confidence in their equipment, durability and price. Players already have their own ways of checking balls, but those methods are subjective and inconsistent. The opportunity is a method that tests a pickleball without taking much time or being difficult to use.

The primary customer and user is the frequent, competitive player; clubs, tournament organizers and other facilities are a second purchaser for shared balls (see Customers). Clubs and organizers need testing to be quick so it does not slow games, and players do not want to wait for someone else to finish testing. The product will be considered successful if it helps players feel more confident about their balls, reduces ball-related problems during games, and does both without damaging the ball or taking too much time.

Requirements, objectives and constraints

Requirements

  • Does not damage the ball being tested
  • Results are repeatable
  • No special training needed
  • Testing does not significantly interrupt a game
  • Works with commonly used pickleballs

Objectives

  • Increase player confidence in their balls
  • Reduce games affected by ball failure
  • Give more useful information than a visual or manual check
  • Keep the product affordable
  • Make it portable enough to take to the court

Constraints

  • Affordable for the consumer and club market
  • Small and light enough to carry
  • Testing cannot take too long
  • Testing cannot permanently damage the ball
  • No laboratory or specialized facility required
  • No claim of USA Pickleball certification [5]

Reading the matrix

Thirty of the 143 possible requirement-to-specification pairs carry a relationship: 15 strong, 15 moderate or weak. Every requirement has at least one strong relationship, and so does every specification but one — ease of use anywhere peaks at moderate, which is worth revisiting, since a specification nothing strongly drives is a specification the product may not need.

What the roof shows

The strongest correlations are structural rather than incidental. Weight ties to all three size-related specifications — paddle capacity, ball capacity and footprint — so any decision to carry more equipment is a decision to weigh more. Time to use correlates strongly with both durability specifications, and user confidence correlates with tracking capability, ease of use anywhere, and mean cycles to failure. In short: the portability cluster and the confidence cluster are each internally coupled, and the design tension runs between them.

How stakeholders weight the requirements

The same eleven requirements ranked by four different stakeholders do not agree, and the disagreements are where the product decisions live. Competitive price is first for the manufacturer and last for other players on court. Improving gameplay is first for the player and last for the manufacturer. "Doesn't damage equipment" is the one requirement that ranks in the top four for everybody — which is why it became a hard constraint rather than an objective.

How the weights were set. Player ranks follow the survey: consistent performance was the top purchase criterion (52.8%) and confidence was low (52.8% rate 1–3), so improves gameplay and increases confidence rank first and second. Each stakeholder's eleven requirements were ranked 1–11 from survey and interview information. Court owners and tournament organizers rank doesn't damage equipment and competitive price highest because testing that extends games costs them revenue and customer satisfaction; the facility owner interviewed (P4) likewise prioritizes smooth operation over ball inspection. Other players on court rank improves gameplay and fast analysis highest because they do not want to wait for an opponent's testing or see balls break during a game. The manufacturer ranks competitive price and long-lasting product highest because they determine whether the product can be built and sold at an acceptable cost.

Where the targets come from

The percentage-based targets come from the survey: 53% of respondents ranked performance consistency as their top requirement, and 53% rated their confidence in self-assessing equipment quality at 3 or lower on a 7-point scale. Both became 53% improvement targets. The physical targets — price, weight, footprint, time to use — were set against consumer-product expectations, because the only existing instruments are industrial and none of them are plausible courtside products.

Why the specifications avoid a solution

Stress, strain and time-between-bounces would all be quantifiable, and all of them would commit the team to a sensing method before concept generation. The specifications are therefore written as outcomes — confidence, failure reduction, time, weight, cost — not mechanisms.

What the QFD concludes

The QFD relates eleven customer requirements to thirteen specifications, weighted separately for four stakeholders [3]. Its main conclusions are:

  • The player's top three requirements are improves gameplay, increases confidence in quality and doesn't damage equipment. They map to the two outcome specifications (decrease in games with malfunction; user confidence) and the no-damage specification.
  • Doesn't damage equipment is the only requirement in every stakeholder's top four, so it is treated as a constraint.
  • Stakeholders disagree on price: it is first for the manufacturer and second for court owners, but last for other players on court.
  • A portability cluster (weight, footprint, capacity) and a confidence cluster (confidence, tracking, durability) are each internally coupled, and the design tension runs between them.

How well supported are the targets?

Targets for confidence, malfunction reduction, time, price, weight and footprint rest on survey data or competitor benchmarking. Repeatability now follows a published measurement-system criterion. Targets for carrying capacity, tracking, ease of use and durability still rest on team judgment and are the weakest.

Primary drivers of success

Customer value comes from malfunction reduction and user confidence. Market differentiation comes from price, weight and testing time, the attributes on which every existing instrument fails as a courtside product. Technical feasibility will be decided by whether the no-damage and repeatability targets can be met within the price and weight targets.

Main tradeoffs

Cost and size versus testing capability. Industrial testers perform more detailed measurements, but they are far larger and more expensive than makes sense for a player or a small club. Our product will likely give up some measurement detail to stay portable and affordable.

Accuracy versus testing time. More measurements give better information about a ball's condition, but take longer. Since other players, court owners and tournament organizers are affected by testing time, the product must give useful results without reducing playing time.
