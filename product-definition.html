Product Definition

The product

A pickleball goes soft before a player can tell, so a ball keeps getting played after it stops playing like a good ball. CuPickle is a pickleball ball-condition tester that tells a player, courtside and before play or between games, whether a ball still performs to standard. Players say consistent performance matters most to them (52.8% rank it first), 36.1% have played with a ball that had gone soft, and 52.8% rate their confidence in judging equipment at 3 or lower of 7 [1]. Their current check, a compression or a look, takes about 3 s but misses softening they cannot feel [2]. The opportunity is earlier detection of hidden softening, with more confidence than a manual check, in a form small enough to carry to the court.

The customer and user is the individual frequent, competitive player: someone who plays at least weekly in leagues, rated or tournament play, brings their own balls, and checks them at the court (see Customers & Benefits). Clubs and tournament directors are a possible secondary segment that has not been validated, so no requirement on this page is set for unattended facility screening. The product succeeds if its grades agree with the USA Pickleball bounce test, it raises players' confidence in their balls, and it does both quickly, repeatably and without harming the ball, at a price a player will pay.

How stakeholders weight the needs

Four stakeholders rank the same eleven needs, and where they disagree the product has to make a choice. The player carries 55% of the weight as the primary user and buyer; the producer 25%, because it decides whether the product can be built and sold at the target price; court owners and other players on court 10% each, because test time affects them but they neither buy nor build the product [3]. Each rank is converted to points (rank 1 = 11 points, rank 11 = 1), multiplied by the stakeholder's weight, summed across stakeholders and normalized to 100%.

Requirements, objectives and constraints

The needs translate into three kinds of target. A requirement must be met for the product to work at all; an objective is pushed as far as the tradeoffs allow; a constraint bounds the design from outside, whether from the course, the courtside setting or the USA Pickleball standards. Each item below is defined and sourced in Table 15.

Requirements

  • Agrees with the USA Pickleball bounce test on at least 90% of balls (S1)
  • Changes a ball's rebound by less than 0.5 in. after 50 tests (S4)
  • Tests a ball in 30 s or less (S7)
  • Grades all five most-used ball models (S11)
  • Result variation below 10% of the tolerance across repeat tests and operators (S16)

Objectives

  • User: raise player confidence by at least 2 points on the 7-point scale (S5)
  • Business: retail at $100 or less (S6)
  • Resolve deformation changes of 0.025 mm (S2); two user actions or fewer (S8)
  • Last at least 1,500 test cycles (S12); 75% appearance approval (S14)

Constraints

  • Producer (course): prototype under the $400 budget; buildable with course fabrication resources in under 8 weeks; finished this semester (S13, S15)
  • Courtside: 2.5 L or less and 2 kg or less, including its built-in rechargeable battery, because wall outlets are often unavailable (S9, S10)
  • Standards: accepts every ball inside the USA Pickleball size and mass limits; no claim of certification or approval [5]

Why a player would choose CuPickle. The user-oriented objective is a confidence gain of at least 2 points on the 7-point scale, delivered by grades that agree with the USA Pickleball bounce test on at least 90% of balls, within 30 s and courtside. No current option offers that combination: the manual check is fast but untrusted, and the bounce test and laboratory instruments are trusted but cannot be done at the court. The business-oriented objective, a retail price of $100 or less, keeps it priced as a player accessory against instruments that sell for $2,900–$4,995 or rent for $299 a month [12][13][14]. Willingness to pay $100 has not yet been measured.

USA Pickleball requirements and how they enter the design

The USA Pickleball Equipment Standards Manual defines what a conforming ball is, so it supplies the reference test CuPickle is judged against, the range of balls it must accept, and limits on what it may claim. Each parameter has one role: a reference test is the ground truth for a target, a constraint bounds the design, and a benchmark is a comparison value that informs a target without being one.

House of Quality

The House of Quality brings the whole definition into one view: the eleven needs with each stakeholder's rank and the resulting weight on the left, sixteen measurable characteristics across the top, their correlations in the roof, and directions, targets, target sources and four ball-evaluation benchmarks along the bottom [3]. Relationship strength is 9 strong, 3 moderate, 1 weak. On the right, each benchmark is rated 1–5 against each need (5 = best).

Reading the matrix

33 of the 176 need–characteristic pairs carry a relationship, 17 of them strong, and every need has at least one strong relationship, so each need is measured by something. The highest-value cell is identifies low-performance balls × agreement with the bounce test (S1): agreement is the operational definition of correctly identifying a worn ball. Deformation resolution (S2) sets the ceiling on that agreement, because wear appears as a small change in how far a ball deforms under a given force [7]. Gives repeatable results is measured directly by S16, a standard gauge repeatability and reproducibility figure, rather than inferred from accuracy.

What the roof shows

43 of the 105 pairs among S1–S15 carry a correlation; the correlations of S16 have not yet been rated by the team. Three clusters emerge, each internally positive, with the design tension running between them.

  • Performance: agreement, resolution and confidence are tightly coupled (S1 × S2 and S1 × S5, 9+). Better resolution supports better accuracy, which supports confidence; this cluster creates customer value.
  • Convenience: test-time spread across operators, time to test and user actions move together (S3 × S7, S3 × S8, S7 × S8, 9+), so one decision about how the test is performed tends to set all three.
  • Cost and size: retail price, cost, volume and mass are positively coupled (S6 × S13, S9 × S10, 9+), with durability the exception (S12 × S13, 9−).

Every strong negative crosses between clusters: accuracy against time (S1 × S7 and S1 × S3, 9−), the central design tension, and resolution against cost (S2 × S13, 9−). Compatibility (S11) is negative against accuracy, price, cost, mass and volume, because accepting the full 2.5 mm diameter range and every ball's stiffness makes the device larger, heavier and harder to calibrate. Limiting scope to the five most-used models rather than all 393 approved models [6] is the decision that keeps the other targets reachable.

Benchmarks: how a ball's condition can be judged today

The first four are also rated in the House of Quality; the load frame is not, because its time, price and size are not published. How the targets will be verified

Four tests will replace the estimates in Tables 15 and 16 with measurements. First, blind grading of 20 new and used balls by hand compression, visual inspection, durometer and the CuPickle prototype against the 78 in. drop test, with repeat grading by several operators, will set S1 and S16 for every method. Second, 50 test cycles on control balls with before-and-after drop tests will check S4. Third, a before-and-after confidence survey with more than 20 target players will measure S5. Fourth, timed first-use trials will measure S3, S7 and S8, and the same players will rate appearance for S14.

Main tradeoffs

Accuracy versus time. Gathering more information about a ball, for example checking it in more than one place to catch a local soft spot, improves agreement with the bounce test but adds seconds (S1 × S7, 9−). The 30 s limit caps how far accuracy can be pushed, and the concept chosen in Downselection sets where the balance falls.

Resolution versus cost and size. Finer resolution demands a more precise and more rigid test (S2 × S13, 9−), which tends to be larger and heavier and works against the 2.5 L and 2 kg limits.

Compatibility versus everything. Accepting every conforming ball's diameter and stiffness adds size, cost and calibration effort. Supporting the five most-used models is a deliberate scope decision.

The characteristics that drive success

Four groups of characteristics decide whether CuPickle is worth building. Customer value comes from agreement with the bounce test (S1) and the confidence gain it produces (S5): together they are the reason to pick CuPickle over a compression by feel. Differentiation comes from delivering that accuracy in 30 s or less (S7) in a tester under 2.5 L and 2 kg (S9, S10) at $100 or less (S6); every existing trusted method fails at least one of these. Technical feasibility turns on whether the test can resolve wear (S2) repeatably (S16) without changing the ball (S4), despite temperature effects, within the cost targets (S13). Product success will be judged by S1 and S5 in user evaluation and by a design that supports the $100 price. These are the targets the Gate Review 2 concept is evaluated against in the downselection.
