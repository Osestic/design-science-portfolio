Section 03

Product Definition

What the team intends to develop, and how success will be measured — without committing to how it works.

QFD Survey n=36 Interviews Benchmarking

From evidence to requirements

The survey said consistent performance is what players buy on (52.8% rank it first) and that invisible damage is their leading frustration (46.7%). The interviews said any new routine has to be lighter than the habit it replaces, and that the institutional buyer may matter more than the individual. The journey map located the intervention before and after the match rather than during it. Those three findings produce the top four customer requirements:

1. Improves gameplay

Fewer games interrupted or distorted by equipment failure. Ranked first by players and by the other players on court.

2. Increases confidence in quality

The player believes the verdict and stops second-guessing the bag. Directly targets the 52.8% who rate their own judgement at 3 or below.

3. Doesn't damage equipment

Testing must not shorten the life of what it tests. The only requirement in every stakeholder's top four, and therefore a constraint.

4. Not a big ask over current methods

Players already bounce and squeeze. A slower or heavier routine will not be adopted even if it is more accurate.

The specifications that decide the product

Of the thirteen specifications, four are likely to determine whether this product succeeds:

  1. Decrease in games with equipment malfunction (target 53%). The outcome the user actually wants.
  2. User confidence in equipment quality (target 53%). The benefit they can feel before the game starts.
  3. Sales price (target $100). The gap between a consumer product and the $2,900–$7,200 machines that exist today.
  4. Weight (target 7.5 kg). The difference between equipment that travels to the court and equipment that lives in a workshop.

The first two define the benefit; the last two define the market position. A product that hits the first two and misses the last two is a laboratory instrument, which the market already has.

Tradeoffs the team expects

The central hypothesised tension is between portability and cost on one side and measurement specificity on the other: a device light and cheap enough to bring to a court will almost certainly sacrifice some of the precision an industrial tester achieves. Quantifying that tradeoff is the main technical question for the next phase.
