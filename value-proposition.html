Section 05

Market Value Proposition

Who will value this, why, what we will build, and whether the evidence justifies going further.

Survey n=36 Interviews Benchmarking QFD

Market size

Pickleball is the fastest-growing sport in the United States, and the segment our research points to — frequent players — is the one the industry already counts separately.

24.3M Americans played pickleball in 2025 SFIA 2026 Pickleball Report [4]
7.48M core players — eight or more sessions a year SFIA 2026 Pickleball Report [4]
18,258 places to play in the US, across 82,613 courts USA Pickleball / Pickleheads location database, 2025 [5]

How we read these numbers. The 24.3 million figure is the whole sport and is not our market — it includes people who played once. The 7.48 million core players are the closest published proxy for the segment our survey identified as most interested (frequent players, 64.7% of whom found an objective test useful). On the institutional side, 18,258 venues is the addressable count for a club-first entry, and it is a far smaller number to reach: a sales effort measured in thousands of buyers rather than millions. Participation grew 171.8% in three years, so the installed base of equipment — and of equipment going bad — is growing faster than any means of judging it.

What these numbers do and do not establish

They establish that the population is large and growing, and they size the institutional segment concretely. They do not establish revenue: we have no conversion estimate, no willingness-to-pay data, and no basis yet for a serviceable-obtainable-market figure. Treat these as the ceiling of the opportunity, not a forecast.

Customer segmentation and business opportunity

Target segment. Frequent pickleball players — those playing at least weekly, who own equipment and keep score — and the clubs, facilities and tournament organisers who buy and manage equipment on their behalf. Within our sample the frequent-player group (n=17) is both the most confident and the most interested in an objective test (64.7% versus 47.4%), which makes it the sharpest early segment — and which the SFIA counts nationally at 7.48 million.

Who plays which role. The user is the player holding the paddle. The purchaser is the player for individual sales but the club or organiser for institutional sales — and both interviewees expected the institutional case to be stronger. The producer would be a new venture or a sporting-goods manufacturer. Other players on court are affected without buying: they wait during testing, and they benefit from fair equipment.

The need and the gap. Players want consistent performance (52.8% rank it first) and cannot reliably tell when their equipment stops delivering it (52.8% rate their confidence at 3 or below; 46.7% name invisible damage as their leading frustration). The instruments that could tell them cost $2,900–$7,200 and weigh 22.8–41 kg. Nothing measures a ball at all.

Why customers would choose it. Against manual checking, it replaces a distrusted habit with a verdict. Against existing instruments, it is the only option that can travel to a court. For a club, the argument is different and possibly stronger: equipment is a recurring cost, and a test that distinguishes a dead ball from a good one acts directly on it.

What we cannot yet claim

We have no market-size estimate, no willingness-to-pay data, and no cost model behind the $100 target. The evidence establishes a real and widespread nuisance and a genuine product gap. It does not yet establish that anyone will pay to fix it, and the portfolio should not imply otherwise.

Market-entry direction

Preliminary direction: a new venture, aimed first at clubs and tournament organisers.

The rationale is that no existing pickleball manufacturer has an obvious incentive to tell customers their equipment is still good — the product reduces replacement purchases, which argues against it being a natural line extension for a ball maker. Selling to institutions first also concentrates a small number of higher-value sales while the consumer price point is still unproven, and gives access to the volume of equipment needed to validate the measurement itself. The main argument against is distribution: an established brand reaches players that a new venture cannot.

Design problem statement

The goal of this project is to design a portable equipment-condition tester for frequent pickleball players and the clubs that equip them that gives an objective, trusted verdict on whether a ball or paddle is still fit for play in the minutes before and after a match, at the court itself. The product must return a verdict in 30 seconds or less, weigh no more than 7.5 kg, sell at or below $100, and leave at least 95% of tested equipment with no measurable reduction in service life. Success will be evaluated by a 53% reduction in games disrupted by equipment failure and a 53% improvement in players' confidence in their equipment.

Scope. The project covers assessing the condition of existing balls and paddles. It excludes designing a more durable ball or paddle, officiating or conformance certification for sanctioned play, and any claim about a player's technique.

Gate Review 1 recommendation

Proposed: proceed with revisions

The strongest evidence for proceeding. The nuisance is real and widespread (72.2% cracking, 66.7% surface wear), it attacks the benefit customers rank first (52.8% consistent performance), players distrust their own detection method (52.8% at confidence 3 or below), and the competitive field is empty where it matters — every existing instrument is an order of magnitude too heavy and too expensive, and no ball tester exists at all.

Why "with revisions" rather than "proceed". Three gaps sit underneath the opportunity: no willingness-to-pay evidence, an institutional customer hypothesis supported only by player interviews, and no patent or standards search. None of these contradicts the opportunity; all of them would change how it is pursued.

The work that matters most next. Two club or tournament-organiser interviews; a willingness-to-pay question in the next survey round; a patent and standards search; and benchtop work to establish what measurement precision is genuinely required to beat a bounce test.
