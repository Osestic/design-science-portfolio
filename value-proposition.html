Gate Review 1 · Customer & Market Research

Market Value Proposition

Who will value the product, why, what we will build, how success will be measured, and whether the evidence justifies going further.

Survey n=36 Interviews n=4 Market data QFD

Customer segmentation and business opportunity

Target segment. Frequent, competitive players who play at least weekly are the primary customer and the users [1][2]. Half of respondents bring their own balls at least some of the time, and 64.7% of frequent players find an objective test useful, against 47.4% of occasional players [1]. P3, a competitive player, expected “diehard pickleballers” to buy one [2]. Facilities (clubs, indoor venues and tournament directors, who must choose balls from USA Pickleball's approved list, Rule 16.G [3]) are a second purchaser for shared balls; the casual players interviewed (P1, P2) preferred a tester provided by the club [2]. The producer would be an existing sports-equipment company adding a new product line, using contract manufacturers and selling through retail and directly to facilities. Other stakeholders are opponents, USA Pickleball, ball manufacturers and the environment.

Market and use context. U.S. open play and tournaments. Most play is outdoors (58.3%) on courts without shelter or power, while indoor clubs supply balls for open play, often with no staff present [1][2].

Priority need and benefit. Every ball in play should perform like a good ball, meaning one that bounces 30–34 in. from a 78 in. drop [4]. Consistent performance is respondents' top equipment criterion (52.8%), yet 36.1% have played with a ball that had gone soft and 46.7% say damage is hard to see [1], and competitive players go through about one ball per session [2]. Because a soft ball earns no replay and a swap needs every player's agreement (Rule 10.G) [3], the primary benefit is consistent, fair play backed by an objective reading; for facilities, a secondary benefit is evidence-based ball retirement.

Competitive design gap. Players judge balls only by looking, bouncing or hitting them [1], in about 3 seconds [2]. These checks are fast but subjective and miss gradual softening. No commercial ball tester exists. Objective testing happens only when USA Pickleball approves ball models [4], and no standard defines when a ball in service is worn out [3]. The team's QFD gives the highest player weights to improves gameplay, increases confidence in quality and doesn't damage equipment [5], and the positioning chart places the product in the unoccupied region that is both objective and fast (Competition).

Why customers would choose it. It keeps play consistent without slowing it down, settles ball disputes objectively, and keeps usable balls in play while removing dead ones.

Overall market size

Global Market Insights values the global pickleball equipment market at $480.6 million in 2025, growing 5.6% a year to $825.6 million by 2035. Balls were $100.5 million (20.9%) of that and are forecast to grow 5.88% a year. North America was 40.9% of revenue, and the U.S. alone $155.2 million [12]. Meticulous Research puts the same market at $1.61 billion in 2024, reaching $3.1 billion by 2032 (10.3% a year), and notes that balls benefit from “the consumable nature of pickleball balls, requiring regular replacement” [11]. The two estimates differ more than threefold, and only summaries were available, so they show the order of magnitude, not a precise value. If balls are the same 20.9% share of U.S. equipment sales, U.S. ball spending is about $32 million a year (team-derived).

The serviceable segment is about 2.4 million players (range 2.1–2.4 million). At the $100 target, a 1–2% share would be roughly 24,000–48,000 units and $2.4–4.8 million, less than a sixth of estimated U.S. ball spending. The facility segment is smaller in dollars but concentrated. P4's ball spend shows a facility version cannot pay for itself on balls saved; for both segments the value must come from consistent play.

Major competitors and market shares

No company sells a ball tester, so no tester market share exists. The equipment market is led by Selkirk Sport (over 7% share in 2025), and the top five, Selkirk, JOOLA, Engage, Paddletek and Franklin Sports, hold about 40% together [12]. These are mainly paddle and ball makers, and they are the likely entrants or partners: Franklin makes the X-40, the outdoor ball whose softening was measured (see Research).

Market growth and trends

  • Participation. 24.3 million Americans played in 2025, up 22.8% from 2024 and 171.8% over three years. About 4.5 million took up the sport in 2025 [13].
  • Core players. 7.48 million, up 20.4% from 2024 [13] and from 1.4 million in 2020 [6]. The customer segment grew more than fivefold in five years.
  • Equipment and balls. Forecasts range from 5.6% to 10.3% a year for equipment [11][12], and 5.88% a year for balls [12].

Frequency and severity of the problem

  • Frequency. 47.2% of respondents meet a degraded ball “sometimes”, and 36.1% have played with a ball that went soft [1]. A Franklin X-40 loses about 10% of its stiffness per hour of play (see Research), so every ball in regular use goes soft.
  • Severity. A soft ball earns no replay, and replacing it needs every player's agreement (Rule 10.G) [3]. Players are not confident judging it: 52.8% rate themselves 3 or lower of 7 [1].
  • Not yet quantified. How many points are decided by a soft ball in a typical session.

Evidence of demand

55.6% of respondents find an objective test useful, rising to 64.7% of frequent players [1]. A competitive interviewee expected “diehard pickleballers” to buy one [2]. Tennis shops already buy a $2,900 racquet diagnostic machine (see Competition), which shows that racquet-sport players and shops pay for measurement. No ball tester exists on the market, so there is no direct evidence of sales.

Assumptions and limitations

The survey shares come from 36 respondents, 80.6% aged 18–24 and recruited through the team's networks, and are applied to all core players. Core players (8+ times a year) are a broader group than our frequent players (weekly or more). The $100 price has not been tested with buyers. Market reports were available only as summaries and disagree more than threefold. The segment is a pool of potential buyers, not a demand forecast.

Further research needed: (1) a larger survey, of at least 100 players including older and tournament players, with willingness-to-pay questions; (2) how often a soft ball decides a point; (3) interviews with ball and paddle makers about selling or partnering.

Market-entry direction

A new product line within an existing sports-equipment company that already sells to players through retail and to facilities directly Interpretation. Strategically, an established brand gives immediate reach to both purchasers, players first and facilities second, and existing manufacturing and support. Competitively, no ball tester exists, so the line can enter first. The host should not be a ball maker: clubs switch ball brands freely [2], so a tester tied to one manufacturer would lose value at the next switch, while a neutral tester can serve all 393 approved balls [8]. For intellectual property, the company could protect the method and calibration data with a patent application, after a freedom-to-operate check against the airflow-tester application found in the patent search (Competition).

Design problem statement

Frequent, competitive players, and the clubs and tournament directors that supply balls, face softened balls that cannot be told apart by look or feel and are found only when they fail mid-rally during open play and tournaments on indoor and outdoor courts. The goal of this project is to design a ball-condition screening product for frequent competitive players, and the facilities that supply their balls, that gives consistent, fair play and lets balls be retired on evidence, in open play and tournaments. The product must give a keep-or-retire answer in 30 seconds or less per ball, work courtside or unattended without mains power or special skill, not damage the ball, and not imply official certification. Official ball approval is out of scope. Success will be evaluated by at least 95% agreement with the USA Pickleball drop-test standard, time per ball, and the share of pilot players and facilities willing to buy at the target price.

Gate Review 1 recommendation

Proceed with revisions

Strongest evidence. 52.8% of respondents are not confident judging a ball, consistency is the top purchase criterion, and 55.6% want an objective test [1]; no commercial ball tester exists (Competition).

Why revisions. The case is strongest for frequent, competitive players, who are the primary customer. Willingness to pay is unmeasured for both players and facilities; the one facility owner interviewed saw no need [2]; and bounce decline is unmeasured.

Most important next-phase work: (1) a willingness-to-pay survey of frequent and tournament players, plus interviews with operators of competitive and league programs; (2) drop tests to set the pass/fail threshold; (3) a cost estimate.
