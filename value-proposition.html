Section 05

Market Value Proposition

Who will value the product, why, what we will build, how success will be measured, and whether the evidence justifies going further.

Survey n=36 Interviews n=4 Market data QFD

Customer segmentation and business opportunity

Target segment. Frequent, competitive players who play at least weekly are the primary customer and the users [1][2]. Half of respondents bring their own balls at least some of the time, and 64.7% of frequent players find an objective test useful, against 47.4% of occasional players [1]. P3, a competitive player, expected “diehard pickleballers” to buy one [2]. Facilities (clubs, indoor venues and tournament directors, who must choose balls from USA Pickleball's approved list, Rule 16.G [3]) are a second purchaser for shared balls; the casual players interviewed (P1, P2) preferred a tester provided by the club [2]. The producer would be an existing sports-equipment company adding a new product line, using contract manufacturers and selling through retail and directly to facilities. Other stakeholders are opponents, USA Pickleball, ball manufacturers and the environment.

Market and use context. U.S. open play and tournaments. Most play is outdoors (58.3%) on courts without shelter or power, while indoor clubs supply balls for open play, often with no staff present [1][2].

Priority need and benefit. Every ball in play should perform like a good ball, meaning one that bounces 30–34 in. from a 78 in. drop [4]. Consistent performance is respondents' top equipment criterion (52.8%), yet 72.2% have played with a cracked ball and 46.7% say damage is hard to see [1], and competitive players break about one ball per session [2]. Because a soft ball earns no replay and a swap needs every player's agreement (Rule 10.G) [3], the primary benefit is consistent, fair play backed by an objective reading; for facilities, a secondary benefit is evidence-based ball retirement.

Competitive design gap. Players judge balls only by looking, bouncing or hitting them [1], in about 3 seconds [2]. These checks are fast but subjective and miss softening and hairline cracks. No commercial ball tester exists. Objective testing happens only when USA Pickleball approves ball models [4], and no standard defines when a ball in service is worn out [3]. The team's QFD gives the highest player weights to improves gameplay, increases confidence in quality and doesn't damage equipment [5], and the positioning chart places the product in the unoccupied region that is both objective and fast (Competition).

Why customers would choose it. It keeps play consistent without slowing it down, settles ball disputes objectively, and keeps usable balls in play while removing dead ones.

Market-entry direction

A new product line within an existing sports-equipment company that already sells to players through retail and to facilities directly Interpretation. Strategically, an established brand gives immediate reach to both purchasers, players first and facilities second, and existing manufacturing and support. Competitively, no ball tester exists, so the line can enter first. The host should not be a ball maker: clubs switch ball brands freely [2], so a tester tied to one manufacturer would lose value at the next switch, while a neutral tester can serve all 393 approved balls [8]. For intellectual property, the company could protect the method and calibration data with a patent application, after a freedom-to-operate check against the airflow-tester application found in the patent search (Competition).

Design problem statement

Frequent, competitive players, and the clubs and tournament directors that supply balls, face cracked and softened balls that cannot be seen and are found only when they fail mid-rally during open play and tournaments on indoor and outdoor courts. The goal of this project is to design a ball-condition screening product for frequent competitive players, and the facilities that supply their balls, that gives consistent, fair play and lets balls be retired on evidence, in open play and tournaments. The product must give a keep-or-retire answer in 30 seconds or less per ball, work courtside or unattended without power or special skill, not damage the ball, and not imply official certification. Paddles and official ball approval are out of scope. Success will be evaluated by at least 95% agreement with the USA Pickleball drop-test standard, time per ball, and the share of pilot players and facilities willing to buy at the target price.

Gate Review 1 recommendation

Proceed with revisions

Strongest evidence. 72.2% of respondents have played with a cracked ball, consistency is the top purchase criterion, and 55.6% want an objective test [1]; no commercial ball tester exists (Competition).

Why revisions. The case is strongest for frequent, competitive players, who are the primary customer. Willingness to pay is unmeasured for both players and facilities; the one facility owner interviewed saw no need [2]; and bounce decline is unmeasured.

Most important next-phase work: (1) a willingness-to-pay survey of frequent and tournament players, plus interviews with operators of competitive and league programs; (2) drop tests to set the pass/fail threshold; (3) a cost estimate.
